the places where the author's own published habits and STYLE.md differ are
collected in section 7 so the drafter does not have to guess.

## 1. Sentence architecture

### 1.1 Length and shape

His sentences are long by the standards of machine prose. A typical
results sentence runs 35 to 60 words; a typical discussion sentence 40 to
80. The length is not padding. It comes from putting the definition, the
qualification and the evidence for a claim inside the sentence that makes
the claim, in parentheses and subordinate clauses, instead of in separate
sentences before and after it.

> "Here, we examine 11.5 years of data from Austin Pets Alive!, a private animal shelter in Austin, TX, which has treated 5127 dogs infected with CPV since 2008." (Animals, Simple Summary)

> "Treatment for CPV in this study varied somewhat based on symptom presence and severity, animal weight (with 5 lbs or 2.27 kg used as a threshold as decided by Veterinary staff), and the result of the most recent CPV test." (Animals, Introduction)

> "This can be due to factors such as the general public perception around larger breeds (47, 48), city ordinances banning ownership of certain breeds (49), housing restrictions implemented at the facility level (50), or concerns around safety, behavior, and compatibility with other home residents (51)." (Frontiers, Adoptions)

Short sentences exist, but they do one of two jobs: they state a step in a
worked example, or they land a qualification after a long one. They are
never a dramatic reveal.

> "Remembering the identity of that item is not necessary to complete the task." (Hippocampus, Discussion)

> "These tests, however, were quite conservative." (Animals, Results 3.2)

> "Time is intrinsically unidirectional. Time is continuous, and motion through it is obligatory. Few other domains have this property, and as such, time may be of special interest." (Dissertation, 1.1.2)

The rhythm to copy is long, long, short-qualifier, long. The rhythm to
avoid is the rejected draft's short, short, short.

### 1.2 Definition by parenthesis

The single most frequent construction in the corpus is a term followed by
its definition in parentheses, introduced by "i.e." or "e.g.". This is how
he defines a symbol, a category, a threshold, or a sample, at the point of
use, without a separate definitions paragraph.

> "At the core of our proposed framework is the classification of three primary types of first-order spatial relations: item-environment relations (e.g., one item is in the upper left corner of the display), item-item relations (e.g., one item is below and to the left of another item), and identity-location relations (e.g., item A belongs in location A)." (Hippocampus, Introduction)

> "In particular, the arbitrary, identity-location relations (i.e. those relationships where the element being bound could have just as easily been anything) are critically impaired in hippocampal damaged patients while location information, disregarding identity, is not." (Dissertation, Abstract)

> "Of the 574 animals that were symptomatic of CPV, 230/574 (40%) developed severe symptoms (where severe is defined as the presence of bloody diarrhea, six or more incidents of vomiting in a 12 h period, white or gray gums, and/or lethargy/coma)." (Animals, Results 3.1.3)

For this paper: "the fraction of the local gravitational acceleration a
one-body model discards (hereafter eta)" is his shape. "We define eta as
..." in its own sentence is not.

### 1.3 Claim and evidence in one sentence

The number, the test statistic, or the sample size follows the claim in a
parenthesis, in the same sentence. The claim is stated in words first, so
the sentence reads without the parenthesis; the parenthesis is what makes
it checkable.

> "Follow up Welch's t-tests showed that hippocampal patients made fewer accurate placements of the correct identity to the correct studied location (e.g., successful identity-location binding) than comparison participants (t(5.21)=5.45, p=0.003;Fig 6)." (Hippocampus, Results)

> "Age and weight were examined directly in the Animal Records (N = 1957; 2016–2020) data via a Welch's T-test and a significant effect of weight (t = −4.287, p < 0.001, d = 0.396) was found, but no significant effect of age (t = 0.815, p = 0.415) was found." (Animals, Results 3.3)

> "In addition, no changes in geographic distribution of animal intake sources at APA! were observed, as measured by a McNemar–Bowker test for multiple correlation proportions (2017–2019 vs. 2020; χ2 = 9.48, ν = 528, p~1)." (Viruses, Methods 2.2)

When a result is the main result, the fraction is spelled out, not just the
percentage:

> "The total survival rate of animals during the study period was 86.6% (n = 4438/5127 dogs survived) with the probability of survival increasing to 96.7% after five days of treatment (with 80% of fatalities occurring in that period)." (Animals, Abstract)

### 1.4 Subordination

Concessive openers carry most of the logic. "Although ..., ..." and
"While ..., ..." open sentences whose main clause is the point; the
subordinate clause grants the obvious objection first. "given that",
"such that", "as", "which" and "though" extend a sentence rather than
starting a new one.

> "Although it might normally be preferable to perform a one-way ANOVA for detection of seasonal variation, assumptions of normality of residuals (as tested via a Shapiro–Wilk test) and homogeneity of variance (as tested via Brown–Forsythe) revealed that this test would be inappropriate." (Animals, Methods)

> "While the role of hippocampus in spatial memory has an extensive history (Hayes, Ryan, Schnyer, & Nadel, 2004; O'Keefe & Nadel, 1978; L. Ryan, Lin, Ketcham, & Nadel, 2009), the present data demonstrated that patients with hippocampal damage are not impaired relative to healthy comparison participants on all types of spatial errors; rather they demonstrate particular deficits in identity-location relational memory." (Hippocampus, Discussion, first sentence)

> "This was carried out primarily to reduce the presence of temporal autocorrelation due to animals being measured on recurring days, given that the disease time course is approximately 11 days." (Viruses, Methods 2.3)

> "This is somewhat conservative given the average length of stay is approximately seven days. This reduced the test variance, but substantially improved the independence of samples." (Animals, Methods)

Note the last pair: the method's cost and its benefit are stated in one
breath, with the benefit last.

### 1.5 Sentence openers and connectives

He opens sentences with a connective far more often than machine prose
does, and the connectives are logical, not rhetorical. Frequency order in
the corpus: "However,", "Moreover,", "Additionally,", "Thus,", "Finally,",
"In particular,", "Of course,", "Note that", "Importantly," (see section 7
on the last), "Taken together,", "In other words," / "Said differently,",
"That being said," / "That said,", "To this end,", "As a result,",
"Consistent with previous work (...),".

> "Note that although this analysis can be useful in understanding expected variability in this outcome measure, the critical statistic was not found via taking the means of the survival rates, but rather via taking the overall number of survived animals divided by the overall population (4438/5127 = 86.6%)." (Animals, Results 3.1.1)

> "Said differently, just as identity-location errors seem to account for a substantial proportion of patients' overall misplacement errors, the disproportionate occurrence of swap and cycle errors at small set sizes in patients can also be attributed to their overall deficit in identity-location binding." (Hippocampus, Discussion)

"We" is used, but the verb is a method verb and the object is data or a
procedure: "we examine", "we take advantage of", "we sought to identify",
"we opt to use", "we review", "we evaluate the degree to which". It is
never "We give", "We argue", "We release", "We show that" as a bare
opener. When he does open with "Here, we", the object is the data, not the
artifact:

> "Here, we present 11.5 years of monthly treatment statistics showing an overall intake of 5127 infected dogs between June 2008 and December 2019, as well as more detailed datasets from more recent, less protracted time periods for the examination of mortality risk, seasonality, and resource requirements in the mass treatment of canine parvovirus (CPV) in a private animal shelter." (Animals, Abstract, first sentence)

### 1.6 How a paragraph opens and closes

A paragraph opens with the general case or the prior literature, narrows
to the specific observation, and closes with either a "Thus," sentence
that states what follows, or a "Future work should ..." sentence that
hands the remainder off. Discussion paragraphs very often open with
"Although [what is known], [what is not]":

> "Although previous work has established effective treatment practices [32,33,34] and discussed the potential influence of signalment factors on survival [35,36,37,38], the implementation of CPV treatment programs has yet to become common practice for animal shelters (though some exceptions exist) [39,40]." (Animals, Discussion, first sentence)

> "Although significant progress has been made in developing diagnostics [27,28,29,30,31], vaccines [29,32,33,34], and treatment protocols [3,35,36,37,38] for canine parvovirus (CPV) since its emergence, few methodologies have been developed to attempt to limit or slow the spread of this disease within vulnerable populations." (Viruses, Discussion, first sentence)

Closers:

> "Thus, in the present study, rather than focus on one type of spatial relational error (e.g. swaps), we sought to identify the multiple types of relations in space, to organize them in a systematic framework, and to determine if all, or only some, types of relations are impacted by hippocampal damage." (Hippocampus, Introduction, paragraph close)

> "Future work should examine potential natural and public-health drivers which might contribute to this seasonality." (Animals, Discussion)

> "Taken together, these data show that across set sizes, patients show specific impairment to identity-location information without showing any significant compound relational error differences once the set size is sufficiently high for comparisons to begin making identity-location errors." (Hippocampus, Results, section close)

The summary paragraph of an introduction starts "In summary, in the work
presented here, we investigate ..." and lists what the sections do, in
order, with "We consider ... We evaluate ... Finally, we discuss ...".
That is the one place where a run of "We <verb>" sentences appears, and
each of them is 20 to 40 words.

### 1.7 Worked examples and analogies

When a distinction is abstract he builds a concrete case and walks the
reader through it, in the second person if the paper allows it. This is
a signature move and the paper should use it for the equal-pull surface,
the noise-filter framing, and the clock.

> "Consider a case where a single item is presented in a bounded spatial environment. Only one type of relational information, namely the item-environment relations, is necessary to remember where that item belongs. Remembering the identity of that item is not necessary to complete the task. However, if a second item is added to the display, each item has an item-environment relationship, but also item-item relations and identity-location relations." (Hippocampus, Discussion)

> "Pretend that you need to return a pair of shoes to Nordstrom, one of several department stores at your local shopping mall. There are many ways that you might remember the location of Nordstrom. You might remember that it is on the third floor at the south end of the mall (i.e., item-environment relation). You might remember that it is one floor below the Macy's (i.e., item-item relation)." (Hippocampus, Introduction)

> "For example, space could be a toroidal ring like we see in video games such as the classic, Asteroids (Wolf, 2010), wrapped on a sphere like we experience on maps from day-to-day (i.e. the earth; Snyder, 1987), in a hyperbolic space (a la a Penrose diagram in theoretical physics; Hawking & Ellis, 1973) where distance becomes asymptotically large as boundaries are approached, or something more exotic (Coxeter, 2008; see Figure 1.2 for visualizations of some of these spaces)." (Dissertation, 1.1)

He also reaches sideways for a principle from another field when it
sharpens the point, and cites it: Noether's theorem for which
transformations preserve a relational graph (Dissertation, 1.1.3), point
set registration for the reconstruction model (Hippocampus, Methods),
inverse transform sampling for scales (Blog, theremin). The paper's
complexity-theory and dynamical-systems nods fit this habit exactly.

### 1.8 Lists

Lists inside a sentence are numbered "1) ... 2) ... 3)" and each item is
a full clause. Numbered lists are for enumerating alternatives or steps,
never for cadence; a three-item list in his prose is three things, not a
rhythm.

> "This framework allows us to: 1) distinguish multiple types of first-order (i.e. pairwise or one-to-one) and higher-order (i.e. group-wise or many-to-many) spatial relations, and 2) determine if these various types of spatial relations are differentially impaired by hippocampal damage by inferring the presence or absence of relational information via observations of different types of errors in a reconstruction." (Hippocampus, Introduction)

> "The reason for this discrepancy is likely a combination of: 1) a difference in mathematical formulation of swapping (i.e. not using accuracy as a prerequisite for a successful swap results in an over estimate of the number of swaps) and 2) the inability of hippocampal patients to represent a sufficient amount of relational information to commit compound-relational error such swaps (...) more than by chance." (Hippocampus, Discussion)

Caveats on a dataset get their own paragraph, enumerated "Firstly,
... Secondly, ... Thirdly, ...", placed before the results they affect:

> "A few noteworthy caveats exist within these data sets that deserve enumeration before examining the results of the analyses. Firstly, in the Monthly Aggregate data, data are missing for the months of April 2016 through December 2016 (though a year-level survival rate exists which matches the existing trends and can be used to assess the overall survival rate). This data loss was due to a change in shelter management platform software and is, therefore, not suspected to create unintended biases in the results." (Animals, Methods)

## 2. How he hedges

### 2.1 When the evidence is thin

The hedge goes inside the sentence that makes the claim, usually as
"may", "in some way", "suggesting", "potentially", or a "though" clause,
and the sentence still states what the data show. He does not hedge by
adding a separate caveat sentence after a confident one.

> "These results provide evidence that changes in human activity may, in some way, contribute to changes in rates of CPV infection in the Central Texas region." (Viruses, Abstract, last sentence)

> "Note that a second CPV season, though weakly supported by these data, was consistent with volunteer and staff reports." (Animals, Discussion)

> "The Mean Absolute Error was lower for the model with two coefficients (23.04 vs. 25.03 for a single-component model), suggesting it is a marginally better fit (i.e., a smaller, second CPV season may be present; see Figure 4)." (Animals, Results 3.2)

> "While the current data cannot provide a definitive response to this proposal, the relevant data are suggestive and considered." (Hippocampus, Introduction)

> "This finding may indicate an overall reduction in infection rates or a delay in the typical CPV season (which was still observed in these data in June, though not in the typical peak month of May [3])." (Viruses, Discussion)

When a whole study is limited, he says so in one sentence and then says
what it does give:

> "The ecological study of CPV infection has many limitations, enumerated above, and cannot conclusively show that CPV infection rates are being modified by human activity. However, the present data analysis takes advantage of a generationally-defining event in the COVID-19 pandemic, combined with seven years of high-resolution historical data on CPV intakes at the largest CPV facility in Texas, to provide a potential correlation between human activity and CPV incidence." (Viruses, Discussion)

### 2.2 Walking the alternative explanations

A discussion with a surprising result walks through each alternative
explanation in its own paragraph, tests it against something in the data,
and closes with a sentence that says how much the alternative has been
reduced, never that it has been eliminated. The rejected draft's "we have
tried to kill it" is the same instinct, told as a story about us; his
version is told as a property of the alternative.

> "Of course, this does not rule out the possibility that a failure to seek care in general drove this difference, a possibility that is extraordinarily difficult to rule out in general. However, if failure to seek care drove the differences that were observed, it might be expected that the dip would persist into peak CPV season or start in a time-locked manner to the drastic increase in unemployment (or other macroeconomic factors) seen in April in Texas and the Austin area [41]. Thus, the transient nature of the observed effect reduces the likelihood that failure to seek care is driving the effect, though it does not eliminate this possibility." (Viruses, Discussion)

> "During the period of 01 March 2020 to 01 June 2020, on balance, Austin Pets Alive! and Austin Animal Center adopted out more animals than the previous year, making this explanation potentially less convincing [49]." (Viruses, Discussion)

> "It is, of course, possible that the significant difference in rotation errors is due to additional memory deficits. (...) This could point to further, more subtle deficits in particular types of relations, but further investigation into this error type is needed." (Hippocampus, Discussion)

### 2.3 Stating a strong result

When the evidence is strong he says so without hedging, and the strength
is carried by the verb ("demonstrate", "strongly supports", "should now be
viewed as") and by the number that follows it, not by an adverb.

> "Taken together, this work strongly supports the assertion that canine parvovirus is an imminently treatable condition, even in persistently resource-restricted environments." (Animals, Introduction, last sentence)

> "Further research should examine these high-risk populations to determine how to best provide them care, but canine parvovirus should now be viewed as imminently and practically treatable within the animal shelter setting." (Animals, Conclusions, last sentence)

> "Although we believe the inferences discussed in this study are on firm statistical ground, some weaknesses in the data, including the lack of treatment-level resolution across the entire time range, gaps in some portions of the data set, and difficulty in measuring every variable that otherwise might be of interest, are worth consideration." (Animals, Discussion)

> "The data bear out this conclusion by showing that if we remove the identity information from the patient data, we see the difference between the groups in overall misplacement reduced by over 75%." (Hippocampus, Discussion)

### 2.4 A correction to earlier work, stated as a method property

This is the pattern the paper needs for the twelve-sample result and the
instrument channels. He does not narrate the earlier mistake; he states
how the new measure differs from the old one and why the new one is more
consistent with what the quantity is supposed to mean.

> "Note, this “swap” is computed differently than our previous version of “swaps” (Watson et al., 2013), as the metric described here includes location accuracy (with item identity ignored) as part of the requirement for being called a “swap”. We believe that this new metric is more consistent with the general item-location misbinding interpretation of a “swap” error." (Hippocampus, Methods)

> "However, the present data demonstrated no group difference in swaps or cycles after accounting for overall location accuracy. The reason for this discrepancy is likely a combination of: 1) a difference in mathematical formulation of swapping (...) and 2) the inability of hippocampal patients to represent a sufficient amount of relational information (...)." (Hippocampus, Discussion)

## 3. How he opens a paper and a section

The first sentence of every abstract and every introduction in the corpus
is about the phenomenon or the field, not about the paper. The paper's
own contribution arrives in sentence two to four, usually introduced by
"Here, we", "In this work, we", "It is the aim of this work to", or "To
this end, we".

**Hippocampus, abstract.** "Hippocampal involvement in learning and remembering relational information has an extensive history, often focusing specifically on spatial information. In humans, spatial reconstruction (SR) paradigms are a powerful tool for evaluating an individuals' spatial-relational memory."
Field, then the instrument. The gap comes in sentence four ("However, the necessity of the hippocampus for other types ... have not yet been investigated systematically") and the work in sentence five ("In this work, three patients ... performed an SR task.").

**Hippocampus, introduction.** "Relational memory is critically supported by the hippocampus (Cohen & Eichenbaum, 1993; Eichenbaum & Cohen, 2001; Konkel & Cohen, 2009; Konkel, Warren, Duff, Tranel, & Cohen, 2008). For example, memory for relationships of items to spatial locations is highly sensitive to hippocampal damage in humans (Hartley et al., 2007; R. J. Allen, Vargha-Khadem, & Baddeley, 2014; Smith & Milner, 1981a; Watson, Voss, Warren, Tranel, & Cohen, 2013)."
The established claim with four citations, then a "For example" that narrows it. Dense citation from the first sentence.

**Animals, abstract.** "Here, we present 11.5 years of monthly treatment statistics showing an overall intake of 5127 infected dogs between June 2008 and December 2019, as well as more detailed datasets from more recent, less protracted time periods for the examination of mortality risk, seasonality, and resource requirements in the mass treatment of canine parvovirus (CPV) in a private animal shelter. The total survival rate of animals during the study period was 86.6% (n = 4438/5127 dogs survived) with the probability of survival increasing to 96.7% after five days of treatment (with 80% of fatalities occurring in that period)."
The one "Here, we present" opener in the corpus, and its object is the data with its size and span. The headline number, with its fraction, is sentence two.

**Animals, introduction.** "Canine parvovirus (CPV) is a highly contagious and relatively common virus that causes substantial morbidity and mortality in dogs worldwide [1,2]. Symptoms include acute enteritis, fever, and potential cardiac sequelae (i.e., myocarditis and myocardial fibrosis), with the most clinically severe disease manifesting (i.e., sepsis and severe dehydration) among dogs infected between six weeks and six months of age [2,3]."
The subject defined, with its acronym, in sentence one; its concrete properties in sentence two.

**Viruses, abstract.** "Previous work has indicated that canine parvovirus (CPV) prevalence in the Central Texas region may follow yearly, periodic patterns. The peak in CPV infection rates occurs during the summer months of May and June, marking a distinct “CPV season”."
Prior finding, then the specific regularity the paper will use. The hypothesis is sentence three ("We hypothesized that human activity contributes...").

**Viruses, introduction.** "The canine parvovirus (CPV) is a highly contagious gastrointestinal virus which, without treatment, has a fatality rate as high as 90% among domestic dogs [1,2]. With treatment, the fatality rate improves to 14% [3], but is still among the most common infectious diseases in dogs with high morbidity and mortality [4]."
Two numbers in two sentences, each cited, each a bound.

**Frontiers, abstract.** "Animal shelter research has seen significant increases in participation over the past several decades from academic organizations, private organizations, public entities, and even corporations that aims to improve shelter programs, processes, operations, and outcomes for the various stakeholders/participants involved in a shelter system (animals, humans, the community, wildlife, and the environment). These efforts are scattered through a huge variety of different research areas that are challenging to define and scope for organizations seeking to start new lines of research inquiry."
The field, then the problem the paper addresses; "This work aims to enumerate ..." is sentence three.

**Frontiers, introduction.** "Animal Sheltering in Western society, in some form, has existed since the mid-1800's (with the creation of both the Royal Society for the Prevention of Cruelty to Animals and the American Society for the Prevention of Cruelty to Animals in 1824 and 1866, respectively) and has been a constantly evolving field to both the benefit (1, 2) and detriment (3, 4), of its stakeholders: animals, pet owners, communities, and the organizations that tie these groups together. In the past several decades, a cultural shift has been occurring in which animal welfare (5, 6) has received more attention, resources, and scrutiny than in the decades before."
History first, with dates in a parenthesis; the balancing "benefit ... and detriment" with a citation on each.

**Dissertation, abstract.** "This work examines the role of the hippocampus and relational memory in organizing episodic memory during navigation and reconstruction. Navigation is a critical component in most organisms' survival."
The one abstract that names the work first; the second sentence immediately steps back to why the subject matters.

**Dissertation, general introduction.** "There are numerous ways to quantify memory efficacy, but when it comes to recollective memory few methods are richer or more detailed than navigation (Eichenbaum, 2017b) and reconstruction tasks (Huttenlocher & Presson, 1979). Reconstruction tasks ask individuals to study some set of information and then, after a delay, recreate the information they observed."
A judgement about methods, then a plain definition of the method in one sentence. This is the closest model for the paper's section 1: a claim about what kind of problem this is, then a one-sentence definition of the task.

**Section openers** inside a paper follow the same rule. A results
subsection opens with what was examined and in which data set ("In
examining the survival rates, we primarily examined the distribution of
survival rates on a monthly level as this data set spans the entire 11.5
years"); a discussion opens with "Although [known], [gap]" (section 1.6);
a conceptual section opens with a one-sentence verdict on how hard the
topic is:

> "Animal behavior is one of the most challenging and complex topics in animal sheltering." (Frontiers, Animal Behavior, first sentence)

> "More so than the treatment of disease, the prevention of disease spread in the shelter environment is one of the most challenging, concretely measurable in the form of infection rates, yet ambiguous (difficult to diagnose in source) tasks a shelter may face." (Frontiers, Disease Transmission, first sentence)

Section headings are noun phrases that name the content: "Time Course of
Survival", "Capacity for Care", "Differences in Misplacement Accounted for
by Global Errors", "Data Problems", "Unique Challenges of Cats". No
sentence-headings, no "X, and the Y".

## 4. Numbers, tables and figures in prose

Rules, each with its instance:

- A percentage carries its fraction the first time it appears, and the
  main result carries it every time: "86.6% (n = 4438/5127 dogs survived)",
  "574/589 (97.5%) became symptomatic", "males dying more frequently at
  (N = 210/931) 22.6% of the time and females dying (N = 114/996) 14.0% of
  the time" (Animals).
- A data set is named with its size and span in a parenthesis every time
  it is used: "the Treatment Records (N = 589; 2017–2018)", "End-of-Shift
  Report (N days = 2474; 2013–2020)" (Animals). For this paper: "the
  frozen tier-2 suite (n = 512 levels, 5 seeds)" is the shape.
- A statistical test is named, then its statistic and p in a parenthesis,
  in the sentence that states the result (section 1.3). A null result is
  stated as "There was no evidence ..." with the statistic, followed by one
  sentence of what it means in plain words:

  > "There was no evidence the overall Population is related to the Survival Rate (p = 0.20; F = 1.692). This indicates that as population increases/decreases, no corresponding increase/decrease was seen in survival." (Animals, Results 3.1.4)

- How a headline number was computed is stated when a naive reader might
  compute it differently (the "Note that although ... the critical
  statistic was not found via taking the means ..." sentence in section
  1.5).
- Missing data are counted, not described: "Note that weight was undefined
  for 444/1957 animals, and age was undefined for 7/1957." (Animals)
- A figure is referred to in a parenthesis at the end of the clause it
  supports: "(see Figure 1 and Figure 2B)", "(Fig. 4 “Original”)", "see
  Table 3 for contingency table for this sample". A figure or table is the
  grammatical subject only when the sentence is about what the figure
  does: "Table 1 presents the 7 key areas identified." "Figure 1 provides
  an additional way of examining these topical areas." (Frontiers)
- A figure that exists only to show raw data says so in its caption: "This
  figure, presented solely to illustrate the raw data under consideration
  in this work, shows ..." (Viruses, Figure 1). Captions carry the reading
  instruction, not the argument: "Darker lines in the death/discharge
  events plot (a) represent animals who did not survive." (Animals,
  Figure 2)
- Magnitudes are given a comparison the reader can picture, in a
  parenthesis: "(a region approximately 430,000 square kilometers in area,
  or somewhere between the size of Sweden and the size of Spain)"
  (Viruses). "(0.86 degrees)" for a significant-but-small rotation
  (Hippocampus). For this paper, a Lyapunov time is "x s, or y episodes".
- Units and dates are explicit and formal: "9.03 h of total care during a
  seven-day median treatment duration", "01 May 2020", "5 lbs or 2.27 kg".
- Methods name the software and the libraries with citations: "The Python
  [15] language was used with critical libraries including SciPy [16,17],
  NumPy [18,19], Pandas [20], ..." (Animals). The paper's reproduction
  section should read the same way, with JAX, the PPO implementation and
  the scripts cited or path-named.

## 5. Register for a broad audience

The blog defines a technical term in three moves, inside one or two
sentences: the term, the reason it is the natural choice, a concrete
consequence the reader can check. There is no "simply put", no "in other
words, for the non-expert", and no retreat from the mathematics; the
mathematics is stated and then made audible or visible.

> "Everything here lives in **log-frequency space** — the y-axis is `log2(f)` for a frequency `f` in Hz. This is the natural space for pitch: octaves become equal-sized steps, because doubling a frequency adds exactly `1` to its `log2`. A perfect fifth is the same vertical distance whether you play it down low or up high." (Blog, theremin)

> "If you treat `t` as a uniform draw from `[0, 1]`, then `S(t)` is a *sample from a probability distribution over log-frequency*. That's exactly what inverse transform sampling is: push a uniform random variable through an inverse cumulative distribution function and you get a draw from the corresponding density." (Blog, theremin)

> "The slope of the scale *is* the reciprocal of the density. Where the scale rises **slowly**, density is **high** — lots of `t`-mass packed into a small range of pitches. Where it rises **quickly**, density is **low** — the scale flies through that pitch region, spending little `t` there." (Blog, theremin)

A dynamical-systems idea is given with a number on each side of the
boundary, so the reader can feel where the boundary is:

> "A daily 15-minute walk (C ≈ 0.2) succeeds from almost any starting motivation level. A 6-day-per-week gym split (C ≈ 0.9) fails from almost any starting condition. This isn't just "start easy" -- it's a geometric property of the system. The basin of attraction for the sedentary state *expands* as C increases, and the basin for the habit state *shrinks*." (Blog, habit formation)

The reader's likely objection is raised and answered in the same
paragraph, without condescension:

> "4GB might sound tight, but the heavy inference work is offloaded to dedicated silicon (more on that below), and the tactical/strategic reasoning happens off-board." (Blog, Bodie)

And the common-sense answer is named as describing the outcome rather
than the mechanism, which is exactly the move the paper's section 3 makes
about the episode clock:

> "The standard advice -- "just be consistent" -- describes the outcome, not the mechanism." (Blog, habit formation)

What to carry from the blog into the paper: the three-move definition,
the number on each side of a boundary, the objection answered in place,
the sideways analogy (Asteroids, Noether, inverse-CDF). What not to carry:
bold for emphasis, em-dash asides, "not X but Y", "it turns out",
"secretly", "the punchline". Those are blog typography and STYLE.md
excludes them.

## 6. Things he never does

Derived from the corpus of four papers and the dissertation; the blog is
noted where it differs.

1. **Never opens an abstract or introduction with the artifact.** No
   sentence of the form "We present <system>". The one "Here, we present"
   opener (Animals) presents 11.5 years of data. The rejected draft's "We
   present spacenav, a simulated navigation environment" is the inverse of
   his habit.
2. **Never uses a short declarative sentence as a thesis or a reveal.**
   The shortest sentences in the papers are qualifications ("These tests,
   however, were quite conservative.") or steps in a worked case.
3. **Never narrates the research process as a story about the authors.**
   No "we got it wrong", no "turned out", no "our own earlier
   conclusion". A change from earlier work is a difference in method,
   stated as such (section 2.4). "turns out" appears in the blog, never in
   the papers.
4. **Never uses italics or bold for emphasis in a paper.** Zero instances
   across four papers. Quotation marks are used for a term borrowed from
   the literature or from common usage (“swap”, “CPV season”,
   “stay-at-home”, “grid-like”) and are kept on that term throughout. The
   rejected draft's `\emph{First}`, `\emph{inverted}`, `\emph{costs}`,
   `\emph{entirely}` are not his.
5. **Never gives a percentage without its denominator** in a results
   sentence, and never gives a test result without the statistic.
6. **Never uses a colon to set up a reveal.** Colons introduce lists,
   equations, and "the following properties:". Never "X: Y" as rhetoric.
7. **Never uses the paragraph-as-headline device** (`\paragraph{Claim
   sentence.}`). Headings are noun phrases.
8. **Never leaves an alternative explanation unaddressed** when the result
   is surprising, and never claims one has been eliminated; "reduces the
   likelihood ... though it does not eliminate this possibility" is the
   ceiling.
9. **Never defines a symbol in its own sentence before using it.** The
   definition rides in a parenthesis on first use.
10. **Never says "release".** Software and interactive material appear as
    a URL in a caption or a methods sentence: "An interactive version can
    be found here: https://codepen.io/kevroy314/pen/jONoXma." (Frontiers,
    Figure 1). "Source code, the full 70+ citation library, calibration
    data, and the Python simulation engine are on GitHub." (Blog)
11. **Never anthropomorphises** the system under study. Patients
    "demonstrate particular deficits"; the hippocampus "is critical for";
    a representation "is maintained". Nothing thinks, believes or wants.
12. **Never uses a three-item list for rhythm.** Every list in the corpus
    enumerates alternatives or steps and is numbered when it is inline.

## 7. Where the corpus and STYLE.md differ

STYLE.md wins in every case below. The drafter should know these are
rules of this paper, not features of his voice, so that a reviewer who
knows his papers is not surprised by their absence.

- **"Importantly,"** opens sentences in Hippocampus ("Importantly, we
  distinguish between a manner of relation and type of relation.";
  "Importantly, however, patients had more placements ...") and a Viruses
  caption. STYLE.md excludes it. Use "Note that" or "In particular", both
  of which he also uses.
- **"It is worth noting that" / "It is interesting to note that"** appear
  once each in Animals and twice in Hippocampus and Viruses. STYLE.md
  excludes "worth noting". Replace with "Note that" or fold the
  observation into the sentence it qualifies.
- **Quotation marks on borrowed terms** (“swap”, “CPV season”) are his
  habit; STYLE.md excludes scare quotes around invented labels. The
  distinction is that his quoted terms are borrowed or already established
  in the literature. For this paper's own terms (eta, authority, the
  equal-pull surface, trivial and nontrivial difficulty) use no quotes,
  define in a parenthesis, and use the term plainly thereafter.
- **A rhetorical question opening a discussion paragraph** occurs once in
  Hippocampus ("What causes this sudden increase in identity-location
  errors in comparison participants at higher set sizes? One possibility
  requires ...") and once in Frontiers. It is rare and always answered in
  the next sentence. Permitted at most once in the paper, if at all.
- **Em-dashes, bold, "not X but Y", "secretly", "the punchline"** are blog
  habits only. Not in the papers, not in this paper.
- **British/American spelling**: the papers are American ("behavior",
  "analyze", "color"). The rejected draft mixes ("optimiser"). Pick
  American throughout, since that is what his papers use.

## 8. Worked demonstration: the abstract rewritten

The rejected abstract (`paper/spacenav.tex`, lines 34–60) opens with "We
present spacenav", runs three italicised "First / Second / Third" beats,
and spends its middle third on what the authors retracted. The rewrite
below follows `paper/OUTLINE.md` section 0 (what the task is, why it is
interesting, what was found) and the habits documented above: phenomenon
first, the contribution by sentence three, definitions in parentheses,
numbers with their n, the correction stated as a measurement property,
and a "Taken together" close. Every number is carried over from the
rejected draft and must be re-checked against the repository before the
abstract is final; `[MEASURE]` marks the one quantity the outline asks
for that the draft did not report.

> Navigating a gravitational field produced by several moving bodies is a problem with a long history in astrodynamics and a short one in reinforcement learning, and the two literatures do not agree on what makes a particular instance of it difficult. Here, we examine a game-scale version of the task, in which a craft with a steerable engine and a finite fuel tank must reach a short sequence of moving targets inside a field generated by up to 32 bodies, and ask what it means for a level of this task to be hard, how that can be checked, and what a learning agent can be expected to do about it. Three ways of picturing the task are developed (a plain observe-act-reward loop; a sequence of chaotic regions that act as noise filters, inside which the aim is to bound the exit trajectory rather than predict it; and a stack of strategies from one-body conics up through learned models), and difficulty is then treated as having two separable components: a representational component, i.e. the degree to which a coarse one-body model of the field is wrong, and a reaction-time component, i.e. the width of the window in which an action must be taken. The first is given a closed form, D = eta/A, where eta is the fraction of the local gravitational acceleration that a one-body model discards and A is the fuel budget expressed in units of the impulse gravity will deliver over the remaining flight, such that D is the share of the tank a wrong model would cost; it requires no simulation and inverts, so that a level can be constructed at a chosen difficulty rather than searched for. The field is shown to be quasi-integrable on the timescale of an episode (Lyapunov time versus the 150 s episode [MEASURE: ratio from the chaos probe]), so where chaos is wanted it must be built into a level rather than assumed. Solvability is established with a reference optimizer, with the caveat that its failure to fly a level is weak evidence (zero successes in 12 correlated attempts is consistent with a true success rate near 16%, the rate observed at n = 32), and the episode clock is shown to be the least informative of the constraints, since a craft stopped by the clock on an energy-efficient path has failed a hyperparameter rather than the task. Finally, a proximal policy optimization agent trained across 20 runs (5 seeds per condition) is used both to evaluate the levels and to be evaluated by them; of four physically meaningful readouts added to its observation, the two that are algebraic combinations of quantities it already observes cost 3.1 to 10.1 percentage points of success depending on the evaluation tier, while the two that carry new information cost nothing, and per-level replay of the trained policies shows how much a single success rate conceals. Taken together, these results suggest that the difficulty of an n-body navigation task is a property to be measured on several axes rather than a knob to be set, and the environment, levels and trained policies are made available so that the measurement can be repeated and extended.

Word count is about 480, which is long for a journal abstract; his run
200 to 300. If the venue caps it, cut the three-framings sentence and the
clock sentence first (they are argued in sections 1 and 3), which brings
it to roughly 330 words without changing the register.

## 9. Checklist for the drafter

Before a paragraph is kept, check it against these; each is a corpus
fact, not a preference.

- Does the first sentence of the section name the phenomenon, the prior
  literature, or the data, and not the paper or the authors?
- Is every new term defined in a parenthesis at first use, with "i.e." or
  "e.g.", rather than in its own sentence?
- Does every number sit in the sentence that makes the claim, with its
  n, its test, or its script, in a parenthesis?
- Is every hedge inside the sentence it qualifies ("may, in some way",
  "though weakly supported", "suggesting"), and is every alternative
  explanation walked and left "reduced", not "eliminated"?
- Does every correction of an earlier measurement read as a difference
  in method, with the reason the new method is more consistent with the
  quantity's meaning?
- Does the paragraph close on "Thus," / "Taken together," / "Future work
  should", and not on a short punchline?
- Is the longest sentence in the paragraph at least 40 words, and is the
  shortest one a qualification?
- No italics for emphasis, no bold, no em-dash asides, no colon reveal,
  no `\paragraph{}` headline, no "we present <system>", no "release".
