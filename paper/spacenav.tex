\begin{abstract}
A craft with a steerable engine and a finite $\Delta v$ budget must reach a
short sequence of moving targets inside a gravitational field generated by
up to 32 bodies. The task is fully specified and cheap to simulate, and it
is still hard to say what makes one instance of it harder than another,
which is the question this paper takes up, because without an answer there
is no way to tell whether a navigator has learned the task or only the
instances it was shown. Difficulty is derived from the structure of the
task as the ways a path through the craft's state space can fail to exist,
fail to be found, or fail to be flown, and the representational part of it
is given a closed form, $\dd = \eta/A$, where $\eta$ is the fraction of the
local gravity that a one-body model of the field discards and $A$ is the
fuel budget in units of the impulse gravity will deliver over the flight;
$\dd$ requires no simulation, inverts so that a level can be built at a
chosen difficulty, and predicts the per-instance success of a learned
policy across 2,388 task instances ($\rho = -0.585$) and within every
evaluation tier, though at the present training budget the prediction is
carried almost wholly by the budget term, since $\eta$ adds nothing to a
model that already knows $A$. The field is shown to be quasi-integrable on
the timescale of an episode, so that chaos, where it is wanted, has to be
built into a level, and the episode clock, which the task does not
determine, is argued to be a choice that confounds the measurement of every
other constraint whenever it binds. An unremarkable reinforcement-learning
agent, trained with five seeds per configuration, is read both as a subject
of the levels and as an instrument on them. It exceeds a hand-written pilot
on trained families and falls short on held-out ones; observation channels
that are algebraic combinations of what it already sees cost it 3 to 10
points of success at a small network and no measurable amount at one twice
as wide; and a training budget three times larger raises success by about
5 to 15 points per tier, which is larger than most of the effects measured.
The environment, levels and trained policies are released so that other
definitions of difficulty, and other navigators, can be tested against the
same instances.
\end{abstract}

\section{Navigating an n-body field}
\label{sec:field}

The motion of a small body in the gravitational field of several larger ones
is among the oldest problems in dynamics and among the least tractable,
with closed-form solutions for two bodies and, under restriction, for
three, and none for the general case since Poincar\'e
\cite{poincare1890,szebehely1967,henon1964}. Spacecraft
guidance has nonetheless managed the problem for some sixty years without
addressing the entire possibility space of trajectories, since a trajectory
is usually designed as a sequence of two-body arcs, each dominated by the
nearest massive body and stitched to the next at the surface where dominance
passes between them (the patched-conic approximation, which treats the field
as locally Keplerian and the transitions as instantaneous
\cite{bate1971,battin1999,hill1878}), and since gravitational encounters are
used deliberately to change speed and direction at little or no cost in fuel
\cite{flandro1966}. Figure~\ref{fig:missions} draws three designs of this
kind. Voyager~2 reached Neptune in twelve years on a flight that would have
taken about thirty without the assists, each of its first three planetary
flybys adding heliocentric speed \cite{kohlhase1977,nasa_voyager_voyage};
Parker Solar Probe lowered its perihelion over seven Venus encounters, each
flyby removing speed instead \cite{fox2016,guo2010}; and Artemis~I reached
and left a distant retrograde orbit of the Moon by way of two powered lunar
flybys \cite{batcha2020}. In each case the design was judged chiefly on
fuel, exposure and arrival, with elapsed time a constraint to be met rather
than the quantity to be minimized, and in each case a large share of the
change in velocity was supplied by the bodies themselves.

Reinforcement learning has a much shorter history with this sort of
gravitational navigation problem. Learning-based guidance has been shown to
recover near-optimal control on particular low-thrust transfers, in
particular in the three-body setting
\cite{izzo2019,izzo2021,gaudet2020,lafarge2021,bonasera2023}, but, much as
the historical designs above do, these reduced cases leave the wider
possibility space of gravitational navigation unaddressed, and exploring it
is difficult in part because it is unclear how the difficulty of a specific
configuration of bodies should be defined; without such a definition there
is no way to say whether an algorithm has learned the broader task or only
the instances it was shown \cite{cobbe2019,kirk2023}. A better
understanding of the task space itself is therefore one of the primary
goals of this paper, and one it accomplishes only in part.

The task studied here is a particular subspace of that problem. A task
instance is a simulation of $N$ gravitating bodies for a time $T$, in which a
craft with a steerable engine and a finite $\Delta v$ budget must fly
through, or match velocity with, a short sequence of targets, several of
which may be moving bodies themselves, choosing an action at a control rate
$H$, so that an instance admits $HT$ decisions. The action space is discrete,
which limits the dimensionality of the control problem
(Appendix~\ref{app:theory} notes the alternatives) and, which was the reason
for the choice, confines the simulation to actions a person could perform on
a gamepad or a similar interface, so that comparison with human performance
remains possible. Figure~\ref{fig:task} lays out one instance in full. The
environment is built as a testbed for reinforcement learning, though the
difficulties reported here are not specific to learning, since a
search-based trajectory optimizer meets most of them as well
(Section~\ref{sec:navigators}).

The rest of this paper returns repeatedly to two ways of picturing the
task, each with its own utility and each suggesting a different notion of
difficulty; neither appears sufficient alone, and part of what this paper
does is to explore both in search of a perspective that is at once
understandable and sufficient for training.

The first treats the craft's flight as passage through a sequence of regions
of differing predictability, i.e. as a state machine whose states are
regimes rather than positions. In a well-behaved regime a trajectory can be
planned ahead with confidence, while inside a chaotic one the exit cannot be
predicted beyond a few Lyapunov times \cite{lorenz1963,grebogi1983}, but it
can be bounded, i.e. the region can be entered in a way that constrains the
set of exits to something a plan on the far side can absorb, which is the
construction that reachable-set and tube-based control make rigorous
\cite{mitchell2005,mayne2005} and belief-state planning gives a
probabilistic form \cite{kaelbling1998}. On this view a chaotic region
behaves as a noise filter, mapping the set of states a navigator can enter
it with onto a set of exits whose spread it can bound but whose member it
cannot choose, so that the plan is made for what comes after the region,
and difficulty depends on what lies after a region as much as on the
region itself.

The second picture is a space of models of the field, spanning from
strategies a person would write down to a policy learned from experience
with little structure imposed on it in advance. The analogy is to
forecasting, where a mean model carries only distributional assumptions, an
autoregressive model adds structured priors about how a series depends on
its own past, and a transformer carries assumptions of a different kind
\cite{box2015,vaswani2017}; the models differ in which priors they commit
to, and a practitioner samples the space at points suggested by prior
methods and at points more general than any of them. At the structured end
of the space for this task sit dead heading (point at the target and burn);
leading a moving target; conic reasoning, in which only the nearest massive
body is accounted for \cite{bate1971}; and the deliberate use of encounters
for assists \cite{flandro1966} and of the field's transport structure for
low-energy transfers \cite{belbruno1993,koon2000}. Between these and the
unstructured end lie methods that learn a model of the dynamics and plan
within it \cite{moerland2023}, and at the unstructured end sits the plain
reinforcement-learning loop, in which the craft observes, acts, is rewarded
for progress, and improves a policy by gradient steps on that reward
\cite{sutton2018,schulman2017}. The space is not a line, since priors
can be added along more than one axis, but each point in it is a
representation of the field, and a level is hard on this picture when the
representation a navigator carries is the wrong one for it. The unstructured
end has a difficulty of its own, since where nearby trajectories diverge
exponentially the objective a policy gradient climbs carries no usable
descent direction unless the discount and control interval sit on the right
side of a threshold set by the field's Lyapunov exponent
\cite{wang2023,metz2021,parmas2018}, which Section~\ref{sec:chaos}
measures. Of the two pictures, this is the one that yields a closed-form
difficulty measure (Section~\ref{sec:eta}).

A level generator that is asked for a hard level has to decide what
``hard'' means, and the two pictures suggest that no single answer exists
\cite{furuta2021,dennis2020,rutherford2024}. Interestingness, which is
usually what a level designer wants, is related to difficulty without being
identical to it, since a level that is impossible is not interesting and a
level that is hard for an uninteresting reason is merely frustrating, and
neither notion can be checked by exhaustion (Appendix~\ref{app:complexity}).
The reason to want a definition anyway is the size of the space. The set of
possible configurations of gravitating bodies is, for practical purposes,
unbounded, and configurations differ in kind as well as in degree, since
each produces a phase-space portrait whose properties (where one body
dominates, where trajectories diverge, where a hazard lies) bear on how hard
a learner or a person finds the task; a scale on which configurations can
be ranked would allow learning to be bootstrapped, for a person or an agent,
through a progression from levels that a simple representation handles to
levels that defeat it \cite{bengio2009,narvekar2020}. This paper is the
beginning of a wider exploration of control in a setting in which the field
itself moves on the timescale of a flight, written as an invitation to
attempt the task alongside the attempt reported here.

\section{Defining difficulty}
\label{sec:hard}

A list of reasons a level can be hard, assembled from examples, has no way
of saying when it is complete, so the reasons are taken here from the
structure of the task. A task instance asks a craft to carry
its state (position, velocity, time, remaining fuel, remaining health) from
a start into a sequence of target sets, in order, and whether it can is a
question of reachability under the dynamics and the available control
\cite{mitchell2005,lavalle2006}. The structure underneath is a graph whose
nodes are regions of the craft's state space, whose edges are the
transitions the field and the engine admit between them, each carrying a
cost in fuel, time and exposure, and in which the task is a path from the
start through each target set in turn whose accumulated costs stay within
their budgets; a free-order tour adds a combinatorial choice of the order in
which the target sets are visited. The ways a level can be hard are then,
as far as this analysis reaches, the ways that path can fail to exist, fail
to be found, or fail to be flown. A path can fail to exist because a budget
is exceeded (the fuel, the clock and the craft's tolerance for damage are
each a cap on the path's accumulated cost) or because the geometry of the
targets makes every path expensive.
A path can exist and fail to be found because the navigator's
representation of the edge set differs from the true one, which is what
happens when a coarse model of the field is wrong (the second picture), or
because the edges out of a region are distributions over exits, which is
what an encounter that scatters nearby trajectories does to the graph (the
first picture). A path can also be found and fail to be flown because the
window in which a decisive action must be taken is narrower than the
controller can hold. A level that is hard for one of these reasons may be
easy for every other, and a repair aimed at one (a larger budget, a longer
clock, a different target placement) generally leaves the others where they
were, so the quantities below are each aimed chiefly at one of these
reasons and are never combined into a single number, and each is a
heuristic for a property that Appendix~\ref{app:complexity} argues cannot
be computed exactly. Exposure, which the derivation names as a cap on the path, has no
quantity of its own here and is measured only through the endings it
produces.

\subsection{The environment}
\label{sec:env}

The experiments reported here use $N \le 32$ bodies, $T = 150$\,s and
$H = 15$\,Hz, so that an instance admits 2250 decisions, each one of nine
actions (three turn directions crossed with three throttle levels).
Table~\ref{tab:assumptions} lists the modelling assumptions and fixed
parameters of the simulation. The simulation is planar with gravitational
constant $G = 1$, in game units (u) and game seconds (s), with a typical
level spanning 1000--3000\,u; bodies attract one another and are advanced by
a symplectic leapfrog integrator \cite{wisdom1991,rein2015}, and the craft
is a test particle (accelerated by every body and accelerating none), so
that a level's body trajectories are fixed by its initial snapshot and a
level can be reproduced from a seed, replayed exactly, and integrated
independently by a browser client from about 1.5\,KB of state. A task instance is a tour of
one to six targets, each a point in free space, a station, or a point carried
by a moving body, with an arrival radius and a velocity tolerance (any speed
for a flythrough; a small relative speed for a rendezvous, which is harder
and is drawn only for a target that moves), in a fixed or a free order. The
craft's $\Delta v$ budget and engine acceleration are drawn per instance
from ranges chosen so that a craft can rarely ignore gravity and fly
straight at its target. Hazards are radiation from luminous bodies,
atmospheric heating, debris fields and radiation belts. An episode ends on
arrival at the last target, collision, destruction by accumulated damage,
departure past 1.35 times the level radius, or the clock; an empty tank is
not an ending, since the craft coasts on. Appendices~\ref{app:obs}
and~\ref{app:goals} give the observation vector and the goal conditions in
full.

Levels are generated procedurally in twelve families, listed in
Table~\ref{tab:families} with the reason each is in the set. The families
are samples from the space of configurations, chosen to span the regimes a
navigator would meet, and no taxonomy of the field stands behind them; they
are the means by which an understanding of the parameters that define a
level is bootstrapped here, since the quantities below were developed by
measuring them on these samples and the families are in turn placed on
those quantities, so that a parameter earns its place by separating
families that were chosen on other grounds. The first such parameter is the
mass hierarchy, i.e. whether one body dominates the field nearly everywhere,
somewhere, or nowhere: three families (\code{sol\_like}, \code{jovian},
\code{saturnian}) are hierarchical systems in which a one-body
representation is nearly always adequate, and three (\code{binary\_star},
\code{trinary}, \code{star\_cluster}) are systems of comparable masses in
which no single body dominates anywhere. The second is what, besides
gravity, constrains the route, and the third is whether the dominant
structure persists through an episode; the remaining families each
introduce one feature along these lines. Testing on families never trained on is the usual means of measuring
whether a policy has learned a task or only a level
\cite{cobbe2019,cobbe2020,justesen2018}. A generated level is accepted only
if it remains well-behaved (no collisions, no ejections, bounded energy
drift) for a validation horizon, and is stored as snapshots at 20\,s
intervals so that an episode may begin at any of several epochs. The pools are 18,000 training levels, 2,700
validation levels from the same families and 1,200 holdout levels from
families never trained on, each validated to 300\,s, plus 1,800
long-horizon levels validated to 900\,s for Appendix~\ref{app:clock}.

\subsection{Representational difficulty}
\label{sec:eta}

The representation a person is most likely to bring to a gravitational field
is the one-body representation, in which the dominant attractor is
identified, its field is taken for the whole field, and trajectories are
reasoned about as conic sections. This is instantiated in the environment by
a function, \code{gravity\_at(topk=k)}, that restricts the craft's felt
gravity to the $k$ strongest attractors at its position and discards the
rest, with $k = 1$ being patched conics and $k = 0$ the full field; bodies
are always advanced under the full field, so that a plan made inside the
coarse representation can be flown in the true field and the discrepancy
observed. With $a_{\mathrm{full}}$ the true local acceleration and
$a_{\mathrm{dom}}$ the acceleration due to the single strongest body, the
fraction of the field that a one-body representation throws away is
\begin{equation}
\eta \;=\; \frac{\lVert a_{\mathrm{full}} - a_{\mathrm{dom}} \rVert}
{\lVert a_{\mathrm{full}} \rVert},
\end{equation}
which is near zero where patched conics is adequate and near one where it is
not. However, $\eta$ alone is not difficulty, because a craft with a large
enough engine can absorb the error. With $\Delta v_{\mathrm{budget}}$ the
$\Delta v$ budget, $\lVert g \rVert$ the local gravitational acceleration and
$T$ the remaining flight time, the authority
\begin{equation}
A \;=\; \frac{\Delta v_{\mathrm{budget}}}{\lVert g\rVert\, T}
\end{equation}
is the budget in units of the impulse gravity itself will deliver over the
flight ($A > 1$ meaning the craft can out-push the field), and the quantity
used to score a level is their ratio,
\begin{equation}
\dd \;=\; \frac{\eta}{A},
\end{equation}
which has the direct reading of the share of the budget that flying the
wrong representation would cost. Both ingredients are available in closed
form from the level snapshot and the task specification, so $\dd$ requires
no simulation and thousands of candidate levels can be scored at negligible
cost, and it inverts, since solving for the budget gives
$\Delta v_{\mathrm{budget}} = \eta \lVert g \rVert T / \dd$, so that a level
can be placed at a chosen $\dd$ by solving for the budget. An ordinary
hierarchical level has $\eta$ of order $0.003$ (Table~\ref{tab:families})
and a median $A$ near $2.3$ ($n = 4{,}096$ sampled instances), so $\dd$ is
of order $0.001$ and $\eta$ has to be raised by construction. That $\dd$
measures what it is meant to was checked by comparing a planner restricted
to the one-body representation against one with the true field on 48
levels pooled into two groups by $\dd$: below $\dd = 0.1$ the two were
indistinguishable (32\% versus 42\% arrival, Fisher's exact $p = 0.74$),
while above it they separated (13\% versus 54\%, $p = 0.005$), with the
coarse planner failing every level in the highest band.

Where in a level a coarse representation breaks is governed by
instantaneous force competition rather than by the field's resonance
structure, which describes what happens over many orbital periods while an
episode here is a fraction of one; the relevant surface is the one on which
a secondary's pull equals its primary's, at $d = a\sqrt{m/M}$ from a
secondary of mass $m$ orbiting a primary of mass $M$ at semi-major axis $a$,
on which $\eta$ peaks at $0.43$ as an angular median over 297 levels
(Figure~\ref{fig:eta}(a); Appendix~\ref{app:equalpull}). The larger lever by
far is family choice (Figure~\ref{fig:eta}(b)): median $\eta$ over the
playable area spans three orders of magnitude, from \code{debris\_maze}
($0.0005$) to \code{star\_cluster} ($0.53$). Figure~\ref{fig:phase} makes
these quantities visible on four real levels, one per family, together with
a single-burn outcome map for each level's own task; the $\eta$ row
separates the families at a glance, while the outcome row does not, because
a single-burn decision space is shaped by the task at least as much as by
the family.

$\dd$ is a first-order measure, and its construction implies a navigator
that spends fuel to cancel every departure of the true field from its
one-body model. It credits nothing to a navigator that exploits the
discarded part of the field instead, such as one that bets on the direction
in which an encounter will throw it and spends fuel only to choose between
outcomes, or one that lets an encounter or a long timescale supply the
velocity change the budget could not pay for, which is what each design in
Figure~\ref{fig:missions} does \cite{belbruno1993,koon2000}. A
level that $\dd$ scores as expensive can be cheap for such a navigator, and
a level it scores as cheap can hide a scattering encounter that $\dd$ does
not see at all, since $\eta$ is an instantaneous force ratio. These are the
points of the model space at which a strategy that adds priors the one-body
model lacks renders a level tractable, and the quantities that follow are
the beginnings of the higher-order terms such a navigator would need. $\dd$ locates, cheaply and in closed form, the levels on which the one-body
representation is insufficient (Section~\ref{sec:correlations} qualifies
this for a learner at the present training budget).

\subsection{Execution difficulty}
\label{sec:trivial}

Separate from whether a representation suffices is whether a correct plan
can be executed, i.e. whether the window in which a decisive action must be
taken is wide enough for the hand that must take it. Measured human limits
(roughly 20\,ms of motor onset jitter for a practiced discrete action
\cite{repp2005}, 200--250\,ms of simple visual reaction time
\cite{woods2015}, and visuomotor feedback responses beginning 120--180\,ms
after a perturbation with a closed-loop correction ceiling near 3\,Hz
\cite{franklin2011,franklin2014}) give the scale, and since the 66.7\,ms
control tick is longer than the onset jitter of a practiced hand, the tick
sets the floor on the time resolution of any action here, for policy and
person alike. Every level is banded on this axis as well as on $\dd$, and the
two are never combined. This kind of difficulty is called trivial here
because it says nothing about the field, and it is not treated further.

\subsection{Chaos on episode timescales}
\label{sec:chaos}

The first picture presupposes that the field is chaotic on the timescale of
an episode, and the threshold at which chaos matters to a learner is sharp
enough that the field has to be checked against it: the
reinforcement-learning objective loses any usable descent direction when the
maximal Lyapunov exponent $\lambda$ exceeds $\log(1/\gamma)/\Delta t$
\cite{wang2023}, which at the discount $\gamma = 0.999$ and the $1/15$\,s
control interval used here is $\lambda_{\mathrm{crit}} = 0.0150\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}$,
a Lyapunov time of 67\,s. Measured with one tick of thrust as the
perturbation and the ballistic factor divided out (the separation of nearby
trajectories grows linearly before it grows exponentially, and a fit that
ignores this reports chaos where there is none; Appendix~\ref{app:chaos}),
the exponent is $\lambda = 0.0045$--$0.0086\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}$ across eight
families (16 levels each), about a factor of two below
$\lambda_{\mathrm{crit}}$, and the uncertainty exponent of the arrival basin
\cite{grebogi1983,mcdonald1985} is $0.53$--$1.02$, i.e. the basin boundary
is not fractal. A Lyapunov time of about 140\,s against a 150\,s episode is
one e-folding per episode, against 10--100 control steps per e-folding in
the reinforcement-learning results on chaotic systems
\cite{bucci2019,rabault2019,vignon2023}. Thus, the field is
quasi-integrable on the timescale of an episode, in the sense in which a
near-integrable Hamiltonian system retains most of its invariant tori
\cite{kolmogorov1954,arnold1963,nekhoroshev1977} and the solar system is
chaotic only over millions of orbits \cite{laskar1989,sussman1992}, and
where chaos is wanted it has to be built into a level, chiefly by episodes
long relative to orbital periods.

Whether the first picture describes anything on the present levels can be
read off Figure~\ref{fig:regimes}, which draws the state machine and then
measures its states, per control tick, along a real flight of the trained
policy of Section~\ref{sec:learning}: contested where $\eta > 0.1$ at the
craft, hazard where the craft is inside a harmful zone or an atmosphere or
is taking damage faster than a tenth of its health per episode, and chaotic
where the exponent measured from the craft's own state, with the ballistic
factor divided out as above, exceeds $\lambda_{\mathrm{crit}}$. On the
jovian flight shown the craft is well-behaved on 55\% of its ticks,
contested on 29\% while a moon's pull rivals the giant's, in hazard on 15\%
while crossing the giant's radiation belts, and chaotic on 1\%, in two
one-second samples at close passes of a moon that may themselves be tidal;
over 16 sampled rollouts the fractions are 53, 27, 18 and 2\%. The
contested state appears on 38 of the 132 jovian tasks of the hard suite,
on contested-family levels it is the whole flight (a star-cluster flight is
contested on 87\% of ticks, in hazard on 11\% and chaotic on 1.7\%), and on
asteroid-field and sol-like levels it is never entered. Thus, on the
present levels, the state that gives the picture its content, the encounter
whose exit can only be bounded, is entered for a second or two at a time if
at all, and the machine reduces to the mass hierarchy, well-behaved or
contested according to whether a secondary's pull rivals its primary's,
with hazard entered from either. On the present levels the picture
therefore adds nothing beyond $\eta$ and the hazard geometry, and its value
is prospective, in that it specifies what a level must contain for a
regimes-based account of difficulty to apply (an encounter at which the
corrected exponent crosses $\lambda_{\mathrm{crit}}$ inside the episode)
and names the quantities a navigator in such a level would need, the entry
set it can afford and the exit set it must plan beyond. The same
measurement records a trap (Appendix~\ref{app:chaos}): the raw 20\,s
finite-time exponent of Figure~\ref{fig:phase} crosses
$\lambda_{\mathrm{crit}}$ on 42\% of the ticks of this flight and on
7--25\% of the others examined, because a twenty-second window cannot
separate tidal shear beside a massive body from exponential divergence, so
that row (b) of that figure is a map of the field's steepness.

\subsection{Solvability}
\label{sec:solvable}

A closed-form $\dd$ says how much a wrong representation would cost but not
whether a level can be flown at all, and for that the most direct evidence
is an attempt with the strongest navigator available, which here is the
trajectory optimizer of Section~\ref{sec:optimizer}. A level is called
solvable if the optimizer arrives or if any member of an enumerated one- or
two-burn family of control sequences arrives, and a level that nothing
tried can fly receives no difficulty label, which is a design decision and
the nearest relative here of the observation that levels an agent always or
never solves carry no curriculum information
\cite{rutherford2024,dennis2020}. Two properties of this yardstick bear on
every use of it. Its failure is weak evidence, since zero successes in
twelve attempts is consistent with a rate of 16\% at probability $0.13$, so
that a level class should be called infeasible only on a sample sized to
the claim \cite{agarwal2021,colas2018}. More fundamentally, the true optimum for a
level is never in hand, since the optimizer is a sampled search over a
restricted control family, so that every difficulty label in this paper is
relative to a navigator that is itself imperfect in ways that cannot be
fully characterized, which is why the paper treats a learning agent as a
second, independent probe of the same levels as well as the thing being
graded.

\subsection{The episode clock}
\label{sec:clock}

Of the constraints a level places on a craft (time, fuel and exposure to
damage), time is the one most easily mistaken for difficulty, since a clock
that ends an episode before a path completes scores as failure a path that
was only slow, and the 150\,s clock used throughout is a fixed parameter of
the environment that nothing in the task determines.
Appendix~\ref{app:clock} collects the evidence that it is short, chiefly
that for the trajectory optimizer on 32 two-target levels arrivals rise
from $5/32$ at 150\,s to $13/32$ at 600\,s before hazard endings take over,
and that roughly a third of the learned policy's medium-suite failures at
150\,s are timeouts. A clock that binds in this way confounds the
measurement of every other constraint, and the position taken here is that
it should be set so that timing out is rarely the way an episode ends,
though lengthening it has a cost for a learner, since the value horizon
$1/(1-\gamma)$ has to reach a substantial fraction of the clock (at
600\,s, $\gamma = 0.999$ and $0.9995$ fail and $0.9998$ recovers the 150\,s
level of performance), and though no finite clock settles solvability in
principle, since a coasting craft in a bounded field recurs and eventually
passes every target it can reach at all \cite{poincare1890}; the appendix
measures that recurrence and finds that hazards supply the stopping
criterion here. What the clock
should be relative to the orbital periods of a level is a separate choice,
taken up in Section~\ref{sec:choices}.

\section{Navigators}
\label{sec:navigators}

The levels are flown by a hand-written pilot, a trajectory optimizer and a
learned policy. The first two sit near the structured end of the model
space of Section~\ref{sec:field} and are the yardsticks; the third is
evaluated on the levels as any agent would be and is also read as an
instrument, since where it fails is a measurement of the levels that no
closed-form quantity provides, which requires a training procedure ordinary
enough that a failure can be read as a property of the level.

\subsection{The reference pilot}
\label{sec:pilot}

The reference pilot (\code{spacenav/baselines.py}) is proportional
navigation with gravity compensation, i.e. a reflex with no plan. At every
tick it forms a desired velocity toward the current target (the target's
own velocity plus an approach speed capped at 35\,u/s and, for a
rendezvous, reduced by a braking law), adds a repulsion from any solid
surface within 25\,u, computes the acceleration that would remove the
velocity error in 0.8\,s, subtracts the local gravity, points the nose at
that acceleration and burns only when aligned within 0.5\,rad. It uses the
true local field but no model of where the field will take it, which places
it one step above dead heading and below conic reasoning in the model
space. On an instance set with a comfortable budget ($n = 384$) it arrives
63.3\% of the time, and collision (21.6\% of instances) is its dominant
failure, well ahead of escape (4.7\%) and timeout (7.8\%), so that the
margins quoted against it in Section~\ref{sec:results} are measured
against a pilot that collides on about one instance in five.

\subsection{The trajectory optimizer}
\label{sec:optimizer}

The optimizer (\code{spacenav/opt/planner.py}) is the cross-entropy method
\cite{rubinstein1999} of the sampling-based kind that runs well on a
graphics processor \cite{williams2017}, applied to the true objective:
hundreds of candidate control sequences per iteration, each rolled out in
the real environment with hard terminations included, with a Gaussian
refitted to the elite set. Gradients through the environment are available
but useless for this purpose (direct shooting with a gradient optimizer
moved the closest approach by a few percent in 250 iterations). A plan is
a sequence of piecewise-constant control segments over the episode (48
segments of 3.1\,s at the 150\,s clock), seeded from the reference pilot's
flight and from an explicit zero-thrust candidate, refined over 96 samples
and 7 iterations toward an arrival objective; the zero-thrust seed is
included because throttle is parameterized through a sigmoid and an
all-coast plan is otherwise never drawn, so that without the seed the
optimizer fails every level on which coasting is the best plan found. A candidate is
scored by the task's cost if it arrives and otherwise by a miss penalty plus
its best arrival gap (the larger of distance over the arrival radius and
relative speed over the velocity tolerance, so that a rendezvous plan
cannot score by flying through the station at speed). It is the strongest
navigator available here and the one against which solvability is defined.

\subsection{The learning agent}
\label{sec:learning}

The policy is an actor-critic over the ego-centric observation of
Appendix~\ref{app:obs}: a fixed self block, one token per body and one per
hazard zone, with the tokens embedded and pooled two ways (multi-head
cross-attention with the craft's own features as the query, and a masked
max-pool), so that nothing depends on the number or order of bodies and the
same weights run on any level. The network has an embedding width of 128,
four attention heads and 285,322 parameters, which is small by the standard
of current general-purpose agents \cite{lee2025}. Training uses proximal
policy optimization \cite{schulman2017} with generalized advantage
estimation \cite{schulman2016} in a fully vectorized loop of the kind that
accelerator-native physics environments make practical \cite{matthews2025},
with 1024 parallel environments by 128 steps per update, $\gamma = 0.999$,
and the remaining hyperparameters listed in Appendix~\ref{app:repro}, for
3000 updates or 393 million environment steps per run, about 67 minutes on
the hardware used. A curriculum
\cite{bengio2009,narvekar2020} ramps the share of rendezvous targets
($0.1 \to 0.45$), the maximum tour length ($1 \to 3$) and, once the craft
can fly at all, the share of levels with targets in the contested
equal-pull shell ($0 \to 0.35$), while the budget and engine tighten from
generous to their full ranges. The observation may also
carry up to four instrument channels that report the quantities of
Section~\ref{sec:hard} to the policy ($\eta$, a log-compressed $A$, a
log-compressed $\dd$, and the local tidal stretching rate), which can be
zeroed individually with the observation shape unchanged; their effect is
measured in Section~\ref{sec:instruments}, and the headline configuration
carries $\eta$ and the stretching rate.

\subsection{Observation channels}
\label{sec:instruments}

The natural expectation is that an agent told $\eta$, $A$ and $\dd$ learns
better, and the four instrument channels of Section~\ref{sec:learning}
exist to test it. Supplying all four costs the agent between 3.1 and 10.1
percentage points of final success depending on the tier
(Table~\ref{tab:instr}; Figure~\ref{fig:ablation}; Welch's $t$-test on seed
means, $n = 5$ per arm, $p = 0.021$, $0.0088$, $0.029$ and $0.0017$ on the
four suites). The cost follows the line of redundancy. $A$ and $\dd$ are
algebraic combinations of quantities the observation already carries,
whereas $\eta$ requires the dominant-body decomposition and the stretching
rate is not present in any form; supplying
only $\eta$ and the stretching rate is statistically indistinguishable from
supplying nothing ($p = 0.40$ to $0.90$), supplying only $A$ and $\dd$ is
indistinguishable from supplying all four ($p = 0.59$ to $0.81$), and the
two halves differ on every suite ($p \le 0.0064$), while the informative
half is nowhere significantly above the no-channel arm. Doubling the
embedding width to 256 (698,634 parameters, three seeds per arm) leaves no
measurable cost (the all-four arm sits $-0.3$, $+2.4$, $+0.8$ and $+2.7$
points below the $\eta$,stretch arm on the four tiers, $p = 0.74$, $0.18$,
$0.38$ and $0.026$), while the wider network gains little on the
informative inputs alone ($-0.6$, $+1.7$, $+1.1$ and $+3.1$), which is the
pattern a capacity account predicts and a conditioning account does not,
so that at a network size where capacity is scarce a channel that does not
change the policy is paid for in capacity and returns nothing, which is
consistent with the view of a policy as a compression of its inputs
\cite{lai2024,kaelbling1998}. Appendix~\ref{app:channels} gives the
argument and the width experiment in full.

\subsection{Per-instance variation}
\label{sec:ghosts}

A suite success rate is one number per checkpoint from a greedy policy, and
it conceals both the policy's own stochasticity and the variation between
instances, which in procedurally generated environments is known to be
large \cite{cobbe2019,kirk2023,agarwal2021}. Figure~\ref{fig:ghosts} makes
both visible by flying every saved checkpoint of one training run on one
fixed instance sixteen times with actions sampled ($31 \times 16 = 496$
trajectories per instance), colored by training update. Early policies
spray across the field and late policies converge into bundles, which are
not always the right ones (on the hard-suite instance shown the trained
policy shuttles between its first two targets and never reaches the third,
in 0 of 496 attempts). Sampled success is far below greedy success (52\%
against 89\% on the easy-suite instance), and the spread between instances
within a suite (60\%, 52\%, 8\% and 0\% on the four shown) is larger than
any effect measured between training configurations here, so that any
claim about this environment that rests on a handful of instances is
fragile by construction. The learning curves of
Figure~\ref{fig:curves} show that a difference between two configurations
read a third of the way through training can have the opposite sign from
the difference at the end.

\subsection{Relations between the measures}
\label{sec:correlations}

The quantities of Section~\ref{sec:hard} were defined to describe a level's
difficulty before anyone flies it, and the test of that is whether they
predict who arrives. Across the 2,388 task instances of the four suites,
with agent success per instance taken as the fraction of four 3000-update
policies (two no-channel seeds and two all-channel seeds) that arrived at
their final checkpoint, success falls monotonically with $\dd$
(Figure~\ref{fig:corr}(a); Spearman $\rho = -0.585$, $p = 10^{-219}$), from
84\% in the lowest decile to 0.2\% in the highest, and the reference pilot
follows the same curve ($\rho = -0.547$; the two navigators' per-instance
successes correlate at $\rho = 0.787$). A logistic model of agent success on
family alone reaches a held-out area under the curve of $0.745$, $\dd$
alone $0.811$ and family with $\dd$ $0.871$, and adding $\dd$ to a model
that already knows the suite, the family, the number of targets and whether
a rendezvous is required still drops the held-out deviance by 1042 on one
degree of freedom ($p = 10^{-228}$; ten-fold cross-validation repeated five
times). Figure~\ref{fig:portrait} draws the difficulty space
these numbers summarize, with $\dd$ against success and the families and
suites overlaid: the suites tile the $\dd$ axis in the intended order

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

Although the task examined here is fully specified and cheap to simulate, it
does not admit a one-dimensional notion of difficulty, which is the first
thing this paper is meant to establish. A learner that treats the task as an undifferentiated
observe-act-reward loop learns it on levels where a coarse representation
suffices and has no particular reason to do better where it does not, and
the results are consistent with that account only in part: the policy is
strong on the hierarchical families, weak on the contested ones, worst on
the held-out families, and also weak on the hierarchical families whose
difficulty is hazard rather than field, which a representational account
does not reach.

\subsection{Learning a class of levels}

The pedagogical question that follows is how an agent should be brought
through the model space of Section~\ref{sec:field}, from the simplest
representation toward the richest, so that what it learns on one level is
the representation it needs on the next, which the literature on
generalization across procedurally generated levels and on learning to
learn across task distributions addresses from two directions
\cite{cobbe2019,kirk2023,finn2017,duan2016,beck2025}. The curriculum used
here is a crude version of this, since the agent learned a one-body
representation first and was then asked to extend it, and whether that
order helps or hinders the present data cannot say. The
literature on choosing which instances to train on next
\cite{portelas2020,florensa2018,matiisen2020,dennis2020,jiang2021,jiang2021a,parkerholder2022,jiang2022}
offers tools, with the caveat that several of its curriculum signals
correlate with an agent's success rate rather than with its regret, and so
miss the instances an agent can sometimes solve but not reliably
\cite{rutherford2024}. The closed-form $\dd$ gives a generator a curriculum axis it can set
directly, placing an instance at a chosen share-of-budget cost of a wrong
representation, so that an agent can be walked up that axis instead of
being shown harder levels in the hope that they are hard in the right
way.

\subsection{Learning over a chaotic simulation}

The first picture of Section~\ref{sec:field} asks for something the plain
learning loop cannot provide, which is a policy that bounds its exit from a
region it cannot plan through. Section~\ref{sec:chaos} says that the present
environment does not contain such regions on episode timescales, which is a
limitation of the environment more than of the picture. Once they exist, the relevant question is whether a policy can learn the
bounding behavior at the region's entrance and the adaptive behavior at its
exit while treating the interior as noise, which is closer to robust and
tube-based control \cite{mayne2005,mitchell2005} than to standard
reinforcement learning, since the gradient through a chaotic system, though
exact, carries no usable descent direction
\cite{metz2021,parmas2018,suh2022}; whether
the learned representation survives passage through such a region would
decide between a model-free and a model-based approach.

\subsection{Choices in the definitions}
\label{sec:choices}

Several of the definitions in this paper are choices. The reference
optimizer is one configuration of one method, so every solvability label is
relative to it; the banding of execution difficulty against human motor
limits is a scale suited to a game; and the twelve families span the
regimes that seemed worth representing, with no taxonomy of the field
behind them (Section~\ref{sec:env}). The choice that bears most on what
the task is, however, is the relation between the clock and the orbital
periods of a level, which two measurements bound from opposite sides. From
below, the clock must exceed the time a path needs, and the 150\,s clock
does not reliably do so, since timing out is a common ending
(Section~\ref{sec:clock}). From above, an episode short against the
orbital period of the dominant pair at a target's radius is the regime in
which the equal-pull surface is the right length scale for a coarse
representation to break (Section~\ref{sec:eta}), in which the field is
quasi-integrable and the learning objective is smooth
(Section~\ref{sec:chaos}), and in which the measures of this paper were
validated; over many such periods, resonance overlap becomes the scale at
which a coarse representation fails \cite{chirikov1979,wisdom1980}, the
chaotic regions that the first picture needs appear, and the quantities
here would have to be re-derived. The window between the two bounds belongs to a target's placement, since
the inner bodies of a compact system and the moons of a giant already
complete several periods in 150\,s while a planet of a sol-like level
completes a fraction of one, and the present levels sit inside it for most
targets and outside it for some. The distinction that matters is therefore between two
tasks. In the first, on the timescale of the relevant period, difficulty is
representational and the clock should be set long enough within that
timescale that timing out is rare; in the second, over many periods,
difficulty becomes dynamical and the present measures are at best starting
points. This paper studies the first, whose difficulty can be defined in
closed form and checked, and leaves the second, for which the measures here
would have to be re-derived, to later work. Each of these choices fixes what its measurement is a measurement of, as
the measure chosen fixes what a curriculum does in the literature on task
difficulty \cite{furuta2021,narvekar2020,rutherford2024}.

% =====================================================================
\section{Future directions}
\label{sec:future}

The definitions in this paper are starting points, and the environment is
released so that better ones can be tried against the same levels. The
clock should be set from the tour and from the orbital periods of a level,
with the discount matched to it, so that the timeout ending is rare and
every other constraint can be measured without it. Levels with
constructed chaotic regions, which by Section~\ref{sec:chaos} means episodes
long relative to orbital periods, would give the first picture something to
describe and would let the question of whether a learned policy can bound
its exit from a region it cannot plan through be asked. Whether $\eta$ becomes predictive of success once the budget is no longer
the limit, i.e. whether representational difficulty ever becomes the binding
one for a learner, is a question that longer training and more seeds could
answer with the measurements already in place. Exposure, which
Section~\ref{sec:hard} names and the paper does not measure, needs a
quantity of its own, since the families that break the ordering by $\dd$
are the ones whose difficulty is hazard. On the navigator side, the model
space has been sampled at only three points, and the interesting ones are
the stacked strategies between them, a navigator that bets on the outcome of
an encounter or waits for gravity to perform a transfer, which $\dd$ cannot
price and which the designs of Figure~\ref{fig:missions} embody; a learner
that plans in a learned dynamics model is the natural first such point. The comparison with human players that the gamepad-sized action set
permits has not been made. Beyond navigation itself, the same
field and the same representational questions arise in adversarial
situations, such as in a video game in which one craft may be attempting to
catch, target, or otherwise react to another, where the moving target is
itself a navigator \cite{mitchell2005,reif1994,dennis2020}. The difficulty measures here will have earned their place if a definition
that predicts success for a stronger navigator than the three tried can be
found, and the levels, the pools and the trained policies are provided so
that such a definition, or such a navigator, can be tested against the same
instances.

\section{Theoretical notes on observation}
\label{app:theory}

The observation of Appendix~\ref{app:obs} is exact and instantaneous: at
every control tick the policy is handed the true position and velocity of
every body and target as of that tick. Two departures from this are worth
setting down, because each would change what a learner could and could not
infer, and because the environment's construction makes one of them
unusually clean to study.

The first is a finite signal speed. If the craft learned of a body's state
by light, a body at distance $r$ would be seen where it was a time $r/c$
ago, and at game scale this is not negligible: with a signal speed chosen so
that the far side of a 3000\,u level is one control tick away ($c \approx
45{,}000$\,u/s, or one level-crossing per 66.7\,ms), a body at 300\,u would
be seen one tenth of a tick late and a body across the level a full tick
late, so that the observation would be internally inconsistent, with near
bodies reported more recently than far ones. A learner given such an
observation faces a problem that the present one does not pose, which is
that the state it is handed is not the state of any single instant, and the
correction (extrapolating each body forward by its own delay) requires the
body's velocity, which is itself delayed. Whether a policy of the size used
here can learn that correction implicitly, and at what cost in success, is
an experiment the environment supports with a change to \code{observe()}
alone, since the delayed state is available from the stored ephemeris; it
has not been run. The formal setting for it is the partially observable
Markov decision process \cite{kaelbling1998}, in which the delayed
observation defines a belief over the current state.

The second is the distinction between a projected observation and a
measured one, and here the environment's design has a consequence that is
easy to overlook. Because the craft is a test particle, the bodies' future
is fixed by the level's initial snapshot, and a navigator that knows the
snapshot can compute every body's position at every future tick exactly,
which is what the browser client does when it integrates the ephemeris
itself from 1.5\,KB of state. The observation the policy receives is
therefore equivalent to a projection from the snapshot, and nothing in it
is a measurement in the sense of a quantity that could have come out
otherwise. In a real setting the two differ: a measured position carries
sensor noise, a projected one carries model error that grows with the
projection horizon, and a navigator must weigh one against the other. The
present environment collapses the distinction by construction, which is a
simplification to be aware of when reading the results, and which the
instrument channels of Section~\ref{sec:instruments} partly reintroduce,
since $\eta$ is exactly a measure of how wrong a one-body projection of the
field would be. A version of the task in which the craft perturbs the bodies
(so that the ephemeris cannot be precomputed) or in which body positions
are observed with noise (so that projection and measurement disagree) would
restore the distinction, and the second is cheap to add.

A third note concerns the action set rather than the observation. The nine
discrete actions (Figure~\ref{fig:task}c) were chosen so that a person at a
gamepad and the policy choose from the same set, and so that the policy
gradient is taken over a categorical distribution, which is the simplest
case. The alternatives are a continuous thrust vector (a heading and a
magnitude at every tick), impulsive burns in which the decision is when and
how hard to fire and the craft coasts between them, which is the
parameterization of classical trajectory design and of the reference
optimizer of Section~\ref{sec:optimizer}, and a set of maneuver primitives
(a transfer, a plane change, an assist) from which a policy selects, which
is the options construction \cite{sutton1999}. Each changes what a learner
can express: a continuous optimum that lies between two discrete throttle
levels can be approximated here only by alternating levels across ticks,
and a burn timed to a fraction of a tick cannot be expressed at all, which
is one of the reasons the control tick is also the floor of trivial
difficulty (Section~\ref{sec:trivial}). None of the alternatives was run,
and the environment supports the first with a change to the action decoder
alone.
