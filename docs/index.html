N-Body Navigation

A craft with a steerable engine and a finite Δv budget must reach a short sequence of moving targets inside a field generated by up to 32 bodies. The task is fully specified and cheap to simulate, and it is still hard to say what it means for one of its levels to be hard, how that can be checked, and what a learning agent can be expected to do about it.

01

Navigating an n-body field

The motion of a small body in the gravitational field of several larger ones is among the oldest problems in dynamics and among the least tractable, with closed-form solutions available for two bodies and, under restriction, for three, and with the general case known since Poincaré to admit no such solution at all. Spacecraft guidance has nonetheless managed the problem for some sixty years without addressing the entire possibility space of trajectories, since a trajectory is usually designed as a sequence of two-body arcs, each dominated by the nearest massive body and stitched to the next at the surface where dominance passes between them (the patched-conic approximation, which treats the field as locally Keplerian and the transitions as instantaneous), and since gravitational encounters are used deliberately to change speed and direction at little or no cost in fuel. The figure below draws three designs of this kind. Voyager 2 reached Neptune in twelve years on a flight that would have taken about thirty without the assists, each of its first three planetary flybys adding heliocentric speed; Parker Solar Probe lowered its perihelion over seven Venus encounters, each flyby removing speed instead; and Artemis I reached and left a distant retrograde orbit of the Moon by way of two powered lunar flybys. In each case the design was judged chiefly on fuel, exposure and arrival, with elapsed time a constraint to be met rather than the quantity to be minimized, and in each case a large share of the change in velocity was supplied by the bodies themselves.

Reinforcement learning has a much shorter history with this sort of gravitational navigation problem. Learning-based guidance has been shown to recover near-optimal control on particular low-thrust transfers, in particular in the three-body setting, but, much as the historical designs above do, these reduced cases leave the wider possibility space of gravitational navigation unaddressed. Beginning to explore that space is difficult in part because it is unclear how the difficulty of a specific configuration of bodies should be defined, and without such a definition it is difficult to say how effective a given algorithm is at the broader task, as opposed to the instances it was shown, with overfitting to a particular n-body configuration the risk that follows. Generalization in this task therefore seems to require, before anything else, a better understanding of the task space itself, which is one of the primary goals of the paper and one it accomplishes only in part.

The task studied here is a particular subspace of that problem. A task instance is a simulation of N gravitating bodies for a time T, in which a craft with a steerable engine and a finite Δv budget must fly through, or match velocity with, a short sequence of targets, several of which may be moving bodies themselves, choosing an action at a control rate H, so that an instance admits HT decisions. The action space is discrete, which limits the dimensionality of the control problem and, which was the reason for the choice, confines the simulation to actions a person could perform on a gamepad or a similar interface, so that comparison with human performance remains possible. The figure from the paper lays one instance out in full, and the values used for N, T and H, with everything else the simulation assumes, are given in section 03. The environment is built as a testbed for reinforcement learning, though the difficulties reported here are not specific to learning, since a search-based trajectory optimizer meets most of them as well (section 04).

Two ways to picture the problem

The rest of the sheet returns repeatedly to two ways of picturing the task. Each has its own utility in describing the task and each suggests a different notion of difficulty; neither appears sufficient alone, and part of what the paper does is to explore both in search of a perspective that is at once understandable and sufficient for training.

picture a

A state machine over regimes

The first picture treats the craft's flight as passage through a sequence of regions of differing predictability, i.e. as a state machine whose states are regimes rather than positions. In a well-behaved regime a trajectory can be planned ahead with confidence, while inside a chaotic one the exit trajectory cannot be predicted beyond a few Lyapunov times, which is what it means for the region to be chaotic, but it can be bounded, i.e. the region can be entered in a way that constrains the set of exits to something a plan on the far side can absorb, which is the construction that reachable-set and tube-based formulations of control make rigorous. On this view a chaotic region behaves as a noise filter, in that it maps the set of states a navigator can enter it with onto a set of exits whose spread the navigator can bound but whose particular member it cannot choose, so that the plan is made for what comes after the region and the region itself is treated as a source of bounded uncertainty. This picture is close to the way a close encounter is treated in trajectory design, where the quantity constrained is the state after the encounter, and it is the one that makes difficulty depend on what lies after a region as much as on the region itself.

picture b

A space of models of the field

  1. Dead heading. Point at the target and burn; adequate when gravity is negligible against the engine.
  2. Leading. Aim where a moving target will be.
  3. Conics. Only the nearest massive body is accounted for, and a flight is a sequence of Keplerian arcs.
  4. Assists and transfers. Encounters used deliberately for speed, and the field's own transport structure for low-energy routes.
  5. Model-based methods. A model of the dynamics learned from experience, and a plan made within it.
  6. The plain reinforcement-learning loop. Observe, act, be rewarded for progress, improve the policy by gradient steps on that reward; no representation committed to in advance.

The second picture is a space of models of the field, spanning from strategies a person would write down to a policy learned from experience with little structure imposed on it in advance. The analogy is to forecasting, where a mean model carries only distributional assumptions, an autoregressive model adds structured priors about how a series depends on its own past, and a transformer carries assumptions of a different kind, about which parts of a series are allowed to inform which, and comparatively few about the form of the dependence; the models differ in which priors they commit to, and a practitioner samples the space at points suggested by prior methods and at points more general than any of them. The space is not a line, since priors can be added along more than one axis, but each point in it is a representation of the field, and a level is hard on this picture when the representation a navigator carries is the wrong one for it. The unstructured end has a difficulty of its own, which arises when the craft enters a region in which nearby trajectories diverge exponentially, since the objective a policy gradient climbs then becomes rough at every scale below the divergence length and carries no usable descent direction unless the discount and control interval sit on the right side of a threshold set by the field's Lyapunov exponent; whether this field crosses that threshold is measured in section 04. Of the two pictures this is the one that yields a closed-form difficulty measure (section 04).

The reason to want such a measure is the size of the space. The set of possible configurations of gravitating bodies is, for practical purposes, unbounded, and configurations differ in kind as well as in degree, since each produces a phase-space portrait whose properties (where one body dominates, where trajectories diverge, where a hazard lies) bear on how hard a learner or a person finds the task. A formal scale on which configurations can be ranked by difficulty would allow learning to be bootstrapped, for a person or an agent, through a pedagogical progression from levels that a simple representation handles to levels that defeat it, and the shape of that progression is itself informative about the task. The paper is the beginning of a wider exploration of control, under particular task objectives, in a setting in which the field itself moves on the timescale of a flight, and it is written as an invitation to attempt the task alongside the attempt reported there.

02

The problem of defining “difficulty”

A level generator that is asked for a hard level has to decide what hard means, and the two pictures above suggest that no single answer exists. The reasons a level can be hard are better derived from the structure of the task than collected by example. A task instance asks a craft to carry its state (position, velocity, time, remaining fuel, remaining health) from a start into a sequence of target sets, in order, and whether it can is a question of reachability under the dynamics and the available control. The structure underneath is a graph whose nodes are regions of the craft's state space, whose edges are the transitions the field and the engine admit between them, each carrying a cost in fuel, time and exposure, and in which the task is a path from the start through each target set in turn whose accumulated costs stay within their budgets. A free-order tour adds a combinatorial choice of the order in which the target sets are visited.

The ways a level can be hard are then, as far as this analysis reaches, the ways that path can fail to exist, fail to be found, or fail to be flown, and the three are distinct. A path can fail to exist because a budget is exceeded, since the fuel, the clock and the craft's tolerance for damage are each a cap on the path's accumulated cost, or because the geometry of the targets makes every path expensive, since targets can be placed far apart, spread so that every ordering is long, or set in regions the dynamics make costly to reach, so that the required cost exceeds any budget the loadout allows. A path can exist and fail to be found because the navigator's representation of the edge set differs from the true one, which is what happens when a coarse model of the field is wrong (picture b), or because the edges out of a region are distributions over exits, which is what an encounter that scatters nearby trajectories does to the graph (picture a), so that nothing on the far side can be planned for. A path can also be found and fail to be flown because the window in which a decisive action must be taken is narrower than the controller can hold. A level that is hard for one of these reasons may be easy for every other, and the repair for one does nothing for another, since widening a timing window does not change a contested field and simplifying the field does not widen a window; the quantities of section 04 are each aimed chiefly at one of these reasons and are never combined into a single number for that reason. Interestingness, which is usually what a level designer wants, is related to difficulty without being identical to it, since a level that is impossible is not interesting and a level that is hard for an uninteresting reason (the clock, as section 04 argues) is merely frustrating. None of these notions, moreover, can be checked by exhaustion.

fig. 3b · the check is the hard part

Consider the most naive possible procedure for establishing that a level is solvable, which is to enumerate every control sequence and simulate each one. The control at each of T steps is a direction and a thrust magnitude, both continuous, so even with a discretization to a distinct actions per step the number of sequences is aT, and at T = 2250 this is beyond reach for any a > 1; the discrete nine-action set used here does not rescue the situation (92250 has more than two thousand digits), and the underlying continuous problem has no finite enumeration at all. Formal results in motion planning make the same point: the generalized mover's problem is PSPACE-hard, and remains so with moving obstacles even for a point robot of bounded velocity; finite-horizon planning under partial observation is PSPACE-complete and its infinite-horizon form undecidable; and reachability for quite simple classes of continuous and hybrid dynamics is undecidable. These are results about problem classes rather than about any single instance, and approximate search is not hopeless in principle, since kinodynamic planning with bounded velocity and acceleration admits a polynomial-time approximation; what they establish is that no efficient general procedure is to be expected, and that nothing about the field studied here, which couples every body's position to the craft's acceleration at every step, places it among the restricted cases for which an approximation is known.

Exhaustive checking is therefore replaced, in practice, by one of two things, a simplifying assumption (a coarse model of the field, a restricted family of control sequences, a short horizon) or a search that samples the space and reports what it finds, and both are used in the paper. Both also share a limitation that a chaotic regime makes acute. In a region where nearby trajectories diverge exponentially, the numerical trajectory of a given control sequence and the true trajectory of that sequence part company within a few multiples of the Lyapunov time, since the shadowing of a computed orbit by a true one breaks down in finite time when the local expansion rate fluctuates, so that a check which reports that a sequence arrives is a statement about a trajectory that may correspond to nothing the craft can fly, and the precision of the check becomes the limit before its cost does. The same literature supplies the premise on which the method rests, which is that ensemble statistics over many orbits remain reliable where individual orbits do not. Thus, in general, a level's difficulty cannot be read off from its definition and has to be measured over a population, and the measurement is itself only as good as the probe used to make it. The rest of the sheet describes the simplifications made to render that measurement tractable, and what they show.

03

The environment

The experiments reported in the paper use N ≤ 32 bodies, T = 150 s and H = 15 Hz, so that an instance admits 2250 decisions, each one of nine actions (three turn directions crossed with three throttle levels). The simulation is planar with gravitational constant G = 1, in game units (u) and game seconds (s), with a typical level spanning 1000–3000 u. Bodies attract one another and are advanced by a symplectic leapfrog integrator at a 1/60 s substep, and the craft is a test particle, accelerated by every body and accelerating none, so that a level's body trajectories are fixed by its initial snapshot, which is what allows a level to be reproduced from a seed, replayed exactly, and integrated independently by a browser from about 1.5 KB of state. Four physics substeps make one control tick, so the craft and a human player both act at 15 Hz. The drawn panels pick out the parts of the environment a learner touches, and the table after them lists every modelling assumption and fixed parameter, each read from the code.

The observation is the craft's own view, with every position and velocity taken relative to the craft and rotated so that its nose lies along +x. A fixed block of 109 numbers carries the craft's state, the tour and the loadout; each of up to 32 bodies is a token of 24 numbers and each of up to 12 hazard zones a token of 15, and the network pools the tokens two ways (cross-attention with the craft's block as the query, and a masked max), so that nothing depends on how many bodies there are or on their order and the same 285,322 weights run on every level.

fig. 6 · the nine actions

The nine actions are the combinations a person could hold on a gamepad, three turn directions crossed with three throttle levels, with no angular inertia and with each action held for one control tick. Four physics substeps make one tick, so the craft and a human player both act at 15 Hz, and the 66.7 ms tick is already coarser than a trained hand.

fig. 7 · the episode and how it ends

An episode ends in exactly one of five ways, tested at the end of every physics substep: arrival at the last target, which is the only success; collision with a solid body; destruction by accumulated damage from stellar radiation, atmospheric heating, debris and radiation belts; departure past 1.35 times the level radius; or the clock, at tick 2250. An empty tank is not among them, since the throttle then does nothing and the craft coasts under gravity alone until one of the five occurs, which on an energy-efficient path is often the clock, and that is one of the reasons the clock matters as much as it does (section 04).

The level families

Levels are generated procedurally in twelve families. The families are samples from the space of configurations, chosen to span the regimes a navigator would meet, and no taxonomy of the field stands behind them; they are the means by which an understanding of the parameters that define a level is bootstrapped here, since the quantities of section 04 were developed by measuring them on these samples, and the families are in turn placed on those quantities, so that a parameter earns its place by separating families that were chosen on other grounds. The first such parameter is the mass hierarchy, i.e. whether one body dominates the field nearly everywhere, somewhere, or nowhere: three families (sol_like, jovian, saturnian) are hierarchical systems in which one body dominates almost everywhere, so that a coarse one-body representation is nearly always adequate, and three (binary_star, trinary, star_cluster) are systems of comparable masses in which no single body dominates anywhere. The second is what, besides gravity, constrains the route, and the third is whether the dominant structure persists through an episode; the remaining families each introduce one feature along these lines, i.e. dense small bodies (asteroid_field), a tightly packed system (compact_system), extended hazard zones (debris_maze), a very massive compact object (bh_cluster), intense radiation (pulsar), and a single unbound body passing through (rogue_flyby). Generating levels procedurally across families, and testing on levels and families never trained on, is the usual means of measuring whether a policy has learned a task or only a level.

A generated level is accepted only if it remains well-behaved (no collisions, no ejections, bounded energy drift) for a validation horizon, and accepted levels are stored as snapshots at 20 s intervals so that an episode may begin at any of several epochs. Four pools are used throughout: 18,000 training levels, 2,700 validation levels from the same families, and 1,200 holdout levels from families never trained on, each validated to 300 s (twice an episode) and carrying eight snapshots; and 1,800 long-horizon levels validated to 900 s and carrying sixteen snapshots, for experiments at extended clocks (section 04).

04

The quantities used

The quantities used to describe a level are heuristics, in the sense that each is a cheap proxy for a property that cannot be computed exactly. The representation a person brings to a gravitational field is almost always the one-body one, in which the dominant attractor is identified and its field is taken for the whole field, and the first three quantities ask how wrong that is and how much the error would cost; the fourth asks whether a human hand could execute the answer; the two after it ask whether the field is chaotic on the episode's timescale rather than assumed to be; the last asks whether anyone can fly the level at all. Exposure, which section 02 names as a cap on the path and the clock discussion below ranks as the most interesting of the constraints, has no quantity of its own and is measured only through the endings it produces.

An ordinary hierarchical level in this environment has η of order 0.003 over its playable area and a median A near 2.3 over sampled task instances (n = 4,096), so that D is of order 0.001: gravity supplies a substantial fraction of the budget's worth of impulse over a flight, but a single body explains more than 99% of the field, so that a one-body model is adequate on an ordinary level and η has to be raised by construction. That D measures what it is meant to was checked by comparing a planner restricted to the one-body representation against one with the true field, on levels pooled by D band (24 levels per band, two bands): below D = 0.1 the two were indistinguishable (32% versus 42% arrival, Fisher's exact p = 0.74), while above it they separated (13% versus 54%, p = 0.005), with the coarse planner failing every level in the highest band. The larger lever by far is family choice, since median η over the playable area spans three orders of magnitude from debris_maze (0.0005) to star_cluster (0.53), so that systems of comparable masses are contested everywhere and hierarchical systems only within thin shells that must be aimed at deliberately.

D is a first-order measure, and its construction implies a particular kind of navigator, one that spends fuel to cancel every departure of the true field from its one-body model, i.e. one that treats the discarded part of the field as an error to be corrected. It prices the cost of flying a one-body model through a many-body field, and it credits nothing to a navigator that exploits the discarded part of the field instead, such as one that bets on the direction in which an encounter will throw it and spends fuel only to choose between outcomes, or one that lets an encounter or a long timescale supply the velocity change the budget could not pay for, which is what each design in fig. 1 does. A level that D scores as expensive can be cheap for such a navigator, and a level it scores as cheap can hide a scattering encounter that D does not see at all, since η is an instantaneous force ratio and says nothing about where trajectories diverge. In the model space of picture b these are the points at which a strategy that adds priors the one-body model lacks renders a level tractable that the one-body model alone cannot fly, and the remaining quantities of this section (the divergence rate, the solvability probe and the clock) are the beginnings of the higher-order terms such a navigator would need. What D does is to locate, cheaply and in closed form, the levels on which the one-body representation is insufficient and some richer one is required (section 06 qualifies this for a learner at the present training budget).

One flight read through the first picture

What the first picture buys on the levels the environment currently generates can be read off the figure below, which draws the state machine of section 01 and then measures its states along a real flight. Membership is decided per tick from the quantities above: contested where η > 0.1 at the craft, hazard where the craft is inside a harmful zone or an atmosphere or is taking damage faster than a tenth of its health per episode, and chaotic where the exponent measured from the craft's own state, with one tick of thrust as the perturbation and the ballistic factor divided out, exceeds the critical value. On a star-cluster level the flight shown is contested on 87% of its ticks and in hazard on 11% (86.5% and 11.8% as means over 16 sampled rollouts), with a chaotic reading on 1.7% that lasts two seconds and sits on a dive toward three stars at once, where tidal shear and divergence cannot be separated in a 20 s window; on four asteroid-field and sol-like levels (16 rollouts each) the contested state is never entered and the chaotic state is entered on 0% of ticks on three of them and 6% on the fourth, all of it within 430 u of the star; and a screen of all 132 binary-star and star-cluster tasks of the hard suite found no well-behaved segment on any of them, since at η > 0.1 a companion of comparable mass contests the whole disc. On the present levels, then, the state machine has in effect two states per family, well-behaved or contested according to the mass hierarchy, with hazard entered from either, and the state that gives the picture its content, the encounter whose exit can only be bounded, is not entered on the timescale of an episode, so that the picture does not pay off on these levels and its value is prospective: it specifies what a level must contain for a regimes-based account of difficulty to apply (an encounter at which the corrected exponent crosses the critical value inside the episode, which by the Lyapunov measurement above requires episodes long relative to orbital periods), and it names the quantities a navigator in such a level would need, the entry set it can afford and the exit set it must plan beyond, both of which are reachable-set computations the environment can already perform. The same measurement records a trap. The raw 20 s finite-time exponent of the portraits above crosses the critical value on 7–25% of the ticks of every flight examined, every crossing within a few hundred units of a star, because a twenty-second window cannot separate tidal shear beside a massive body from exponential divergence (along these flights its logarithm tracks the tidal closed form √(∑b 2GMb/rb3) at correlations of 0.86–1.00), so that read with that exponent the near field of every star is chaotic, which is the ballistic-drift error in another guise; row (b) of the portraits should accordingly be read as a map of the field's steepness, and where the field is unpredictable is the separate question that the ballistic correction answers.

The episode clock

Of the constraints a level places on a craft (time, fuel, and exposure to damage), time is the least interesting, and it is also the one most easily mistaken for difficulty, since the clock mostly penalizes a craft that chooses an energy-efficient path over a fast one. This is a position, and the measurements below are what support it. The optimizer flown on 32 two-target levels at four clocks arrives on 5/32 at 150 s, 8/32 at 300 s and 13/32 at 600 s, then 11/32 at 900 s, while timeouts fall from 18 to 5 and hazard endings rise from 9 to 16, so that of the thirteen timeouts a longer clock removes, about half become arrivals and half become hazard endings. The scripted reflex pilot on 600 s suites gives the opposite reading, its 150 s timeouts becoming escapes and collisions at 600 s rather than arrivals (medium suite: timeouts 102 to 17, escapes 53 to 105, arrivals 206 to 197), and a learned policy trained at 600 s is a third case (section 06). A longer clock gives a navigator the time to finish a path it could not otherwise finish, or the time to spend its fuel and fall into a body, and which of the two it gets depends on the navigator and not on the clock.

There is also a reason the clock cannot simply be set long enough to settle the question. A bounded gravitational system is recurrent, in the sense Poincaré established for exactly this problem, so a craft that never thrusts will, given enough time, pass within the arrival radius of any target it can reach at all, and a finite clock is therefore a definition of the task rather than a measurement of it. Measured directly, coasting from the start of every task in the 600 s suites for 900 s, the craft passes within the arrival radius of some target on 2.3–5.1% of tasks by 150 s and 5.3–9.8% by 900 s, still rising at the end, while over the same interval the fraction of tasks ending in collision, destruction or escape rises to 26–27% on the three harder tiers; on a pool of 900 levels validated to 9,000 s, contact reaches 8.7–12.9% and then goes flat, because by then 78–80% of the non-easy tasks have ended and the craft is dead. The race between the improbable path and exposure is won by exposure, and the hazards supply the stopping criterion that recurrence does not.

What a single success rate hides

A suite success rate is one number per checkpoint, computed from a greedy policy that takes its most probable action, and it conceals both the policy's own stochasticity and the variation between individual task instances. Below, every saved checkpoint of one training run flies one fixed instance sixteen times with actions sampled rather than maximized (31 × 16 = 496 trajectories per instance), colored by the training update at which each attempt was flown. Early policies spray across the field and late policies converge into bundles, which are not always the right ones. Sampled success is far below greedy success (the easy instance shown arrives 52% of the time when sampled, against 89% for the greedy policy on that suite), and the spread between instances within a suite (60%, 52%, 8% and 0% on the four shown) is larger than any effect measured between training configurations in the paper, since the channel effect of section 06 is seven to ten points and two instances drawn from the same suite differ by fifty.

Against the reference pilot, at two training budgets

Every configuration compared in the paper was trained for 3000 updates, and whether that budget is where the comparisons should be made was answered by training the best configuration (the policy told η and the stretching rate, for reasons given below) for 10,000 updates, or 1.31 billion steps, at two seeds. Telling the learner its own difficulty

Having defined η, A and D as the quantities that describe a level's difficulty, the natural expectation is that an agent told those quantities learns better, and four channels were added to the observation on that expectation: η, a log-compressed authority A, a log-compressed demand D, and the local tidal stretching rate (how fast nearby trajectories are pulled apart). Supplying all four costs the agent between 3.1 and 10.1 percentage points of final success depending on the tier (Welch's t-test on seed means, n = 5 per arm, p = 0.021, 0.0088, 0.029 and 0.0017 on the easy, medium, hard and blind suites). The observation already carries the local gravity, the product of fuel and engine acceleration, and the fraction of the episode remaining, so that A is an algebraic combination of three quantities the agent already has and D is redundant given η and A, whereas η requires the dominant-body decomposition and the stretching rate is not present in any form. Splitting the panel along that line puts the entire cost on the redundant half, since supplying only η and the stretching rate is statistically indistinguishable from supplying nothing (p = 0.40 to 0.90 across suites), supplying only A and D is indistinguishable from supplying all four (p = 0.59 to 0.81), and the contrast between the two halves is significant on every suite (p ≤ 0.0064). In this environment, at this network size and step budget, an observation channel's value to a learner depends on whether it is new rather than on whether it is true; the mechanism of the cost is not isolated.

A third case, trained at a 600 s clock

Three seeds of the best 150 s configuration were trained for 3000 updates with the clock at 600 s on the long-horizon pool and evaluated on 600 s suites (same families and task specifications as the 150 s suites, different levels; and the blind suite is not blind at this clock, since no long-horizon pool carries the holdout families). Two seeds learned and one did not: the two that learned reached 68% and 74% on the easy suite and 26% and 32% on the medium suite, against 90% and 42% for the same configuration at 150 s, while the third never exceeded 19% on the easy suite and finished at 4%, so that the mean over seeds is 48.9 ± 38.6, 19.8 ± 16.1, 3.9 ± 3.6 and 13.1 ± 10.5 on the four tiers. Note that the dominant ending for the policies that did learn was still the clock, with 37–42% of medium-suite tasks timing out at 600 s against roughly a third at 150 s, so that four times the time produced more timeouts. The likely explanation is the discount, which at γ = 0.999 gives the value estimate a horizon of about 65 s, a reasonable fraction of a 150 s episode and a ninth of a 600 s one, so that a policy trained at the longer clock is rewarded for progress it cannot see the end of and is not penalized for drifting. Raising the discount to match the clock confirms this, and the threshold is sharp: at γ = 0.9995 (about 133 s of lookahead) the failure reproduces almost exactly, with the same seed never learning, while at γ = 0.9998 (about 333 s) all three seeds learn, reaching 83.7 / 42.4 / 14.1 / 29.7 with seed spread under 1.5 points, at or above the 150 s reference on medium, hard and blind. Thus, the clock and the discount behave as a single setting, and a longer clock is available to a learner only together with a horizon that reaches a substantial fraction of it.

Do the quantities predict who arrives?

The quantities of section 04 were defined to describe a level's difficulty before anyone flies it, and the test of that is whether they predict who arrives. Across the 2,388 task instances of the four 150 s suites, with agent success per instance taken as the fraction of four 3000-update policies that arrived at their final checkpoint, success falls monotonically with D (Spearman ρ = −0.585, p = 10−219), from 84% in the lowest decile to 0.2% in the highest, and the reference pilot follows the same curve (ρ = −0.547; the two navigators' per-instance successes correlate at ρ = 0.787). A logistic model of agent success on family alone reaches a held-out area under the curve of 0.745, D alone reaches 0.811 and family with D reaches 0.871, and adding D to a model that already knows the suite, the family, the number of targets and whether a rendezvous is required still drops the held-out deviance by 1042 on one degree of freedom. Thus, D predicts success better than family alone and better than the suite's own design levers, as it was meant to.

The figure below draws the difficulty space these numbers summarize, D against the policy's per-instance success with the families and suites overlaid. The suites tile the D axis in the intended order (median D of 0.0005, 0.0091, 0.023 and 0.044 on easy, medium, hard and blind) and success falls with D within each of them (ρ = −0.37 to −0.48, every p < 10−18), so the pooled relation is not an artifact of the suites' composition. The hierarchical families occupy half a decade of median D (0.0007–0.0037) at success rates from 25% to 73%, which is the hazard effect of the next paragraph seen from above, and the contested families sit two to three decades to the right (0.29–1.45) at 17–22%. One feature of the portrait is not pressed here: where the two groups overlap in D (roughly 0.03–0.1) the contested families' curves fall through 50% at D of 0.04–0.17, against 0.0014–0.014 for sol_like, jovian and saturnian, as if a given share-of-budget cost were easier to absorb in a contested field than in a hierarchical one. The overlap is narrow and is confounded with suite composition, since no contested family appears in the easy suite, and a within-suite comparison would be needed before reading it as a property of the families.

However, D does so for the wrong half of itself. Separating the ratio into its parts, the authority A alone is the stronger predictor (ρ = +0.754; held-out area 0.946 with the design levers), while η at the targets is weak (ρ = −0.248) and, once A is in the model, carries no information at all (p = 0.67). Doubling the network's embedding width to 256 settles what the cost was: it falls to −0.3 / +2.4 / +0.8 / +2.7 points, the all-four arm at the wider network matches the η,stretch arm at either width, and the wider network gains little on good inputs. The redundant channels cost capacity, not conditioning. The relation between success and η is in fact non-monotone, peaking near η ≈ 0.005 and falling on both sides, and the low-η failures are the hazard families (debris_maze 25%, asteroid_field 28%, compact_system 31%, pulsar 39%), where the field is simple and the route is not. What the learned policy's success tracks at this training budget is the Δv budget against the tour, i.e. whether a direct flight is affordable, and the representational component of difficulty that D was built to capture is not yet the component that limits the learner; whether η becomes predictive once A is no longer the limit is a question the 10,000-update policies could answer and the present ones cannot. At the level of families the picture is the same from the other side, since a family's median D predicts its success rate (ρ = −0.734 over the twelve families, p = 0.007) and the families that break the ordering are the ones whose difficulty is hazard rather than field.

How to try it

  • One repository. Environment, level generators, evaluation suites, trained policies and the analysis behind every figure: kevroy314/spaceship-navigator.
  • Figures regenerate from stored measurements. scripts/paper_figures.py, scripts/fig_task.py, scripts/fig_phase.py, scripts/noop_clock.py and scripts/correlations.py read stored results rather than numbers typed into the source, so a claim and its figure cannot drift apart.
  • Pools are reproducible from seeds. scripts/build_pools.py rebuilds the 18,000-level training pool and the validation, holdout and long-horizon pools from their seed blocks, so the levels are reproducible without being distributed.
  • The reasoning is on the record. docs/decisions/ is a numbered, append-only series of design decisions with the measurement behind each; a reversed decision is superseded by a new record rather than edited.
  • Fly it yourself. The browser client under web/ is a direct port of the physics, and a parity test holds the two integrators to the same trajectories, which is what makes the replays on sheet 2 the flights that were scored rather than re-renderings of them.

Companion sheet to a draft paper; the paper is the source for every number here. The environment, the experiments and the analysis were developed in collaboration with Claude (Anthropic), operating as an autonomous agent with access to the repository and compute.
